\section{A Debt of Gratitude}

When Leibniz and Newton independently invented the calculus, they gave
humanity something close to a miracle: the ability to describe change
itself. Curves, velocities, forces---everything that moves could suddenly
be written in the language of the continuum, and the universe answered
back with extraordinary clarity.

Riemann deepened the gift. His geometry allowed curvature to vary from
point to point, freeing mathematics from the flat stage of Euclid.
A century later, Einstein recognised that Riemann's flexible fabric was
exactly the right language for gravity: mass tells spacetime how to
curve, spacetime tells mass how to move. General Relativity, built
entirely on the smooth manifold, predicted the bending of starlight,
the precession of Mercury, gravitational waves, and black holes---all
confirmed by experiment with breathtaking precision.

No honest account of physics can begin without acknowledging this.
The continuum has been, for three hundred years, among the most
successful ideas in the history of science.

Yet every great framework eventually meets a horizon---a regime where
its own internal logic points beyond itself. For Newtonian mechanics it
was the speed of light; for classical electrodynamics it was the
ultraviolet catastrophe. For the continuum, the horizon appears at the
Planck scale.

\section{The Horizon of Smoothness}

Imagine a beach. From a mile away, the sand looks like a smooth,
continuous golden sheet. But if you kneel down and look closely, the
continuity gives way to individual grains, jagged and discrete. There is
no ``half-grain''; there is sand, or there is gap.

General Relativity models spacetime as a smooth manifold---a continuum
that can be zoomed into without limit. Quantum Mechanics, however,
introduces a complementary insight: action is quantised ($\hbar$).
Measuring a position with infinite precision requires infinite
energy---enough to form a black hole that swallows the experiment. This
implies a natural limit to resolution, a ``pixel size'' for reality:
the Planck length $\lP \approx 1.6 \times 10^{-35}$ m \cite{planck1899,garay1995}.

If spacetime has a minimum length, the continuum description, however
powerful, is an approximation. This book explores the consequences of
taking that observation seriously. We ask whether spacetime admits a
deeper, discrete description from which the continuum emerges as a
large-scale limit, just as the smooth golden beach emerges from billions
of grains.

\section{The Lattice Paradox}

\smallskip\noindent
\textit{Emma's thought experiment.}
Emma tiles her kitchen floor with square tiles and walks diagonally
across the room. She can feel every grout line under her feet---the grid
betrays a ``special'' direction. Then she scatters a handful of rice
grains randomly on the same floor. Walking again, she finds no preferred
direction: no matter which way she moves, the rice looks the same.
Regular grids have built-in directions; random sprinklings do not. This
is the lattice paradox in miniature.
\smallskip

\noindent
The most natural first guess is to replace the smooth manifold with a
regular grid. This approach has proven fruitful in lattice QCD. But for
fundamental spacetime, a lattice encounters a specific difficulty: it
conflicts with \textbf{Lorentz Invariance}.

\begin{definition}[Lorentz Invariance]
The laws of physics are the same for all observers moving at constant
velocity. There is no preferred frame of rest.
\end{definition}

\noindent
If spacetime were a grid, the grid lines would define preferred
directions. An observer moving along them sees something different from
one moving diagonally. The grid also defines a preferred rest frame.

Consider 2D Minkowski spacetime tessellated by a square lattice with
spacing $\ell$ and density $\rho = 1/\ell^2$. An observer moving at
velocity $v$ sees lengths contracted by $\gamma = (1 - v^2)^{-1/2}$:
\[
  \rho' = \gamma\,\rho.
\]
At $v = 0.99c$, this observer sees \emph{seven times more} spacetime
events per unit volume---absolute motion has been detected. The regular
grid is not relativistic.

\section{Stochastic Discreteness: The Next Step}

The resolution is not to abandon discreteness, but to add the one
ingredient the lattice lacked: \textbf{randomness}. A regular lattice has
preferred directions; a random distribution does not. Consider a
\textbf{Poisson Process}---the same process that describes radioactive
decay. The probability of finding $N$ points in a spacetime volume $V$
depends only on $V$ and a fundamental density $\rho$:
\begin{equation}
  P(N) = \frac{(\rho V)^N e^{-\rho V}}{N!}
\end{equation}
A Lorentz boost preserves 4-volume ($\det\Lambda = 1$), and the Poisson
distribution depends only on $V$. Therefore $P'(N) = P(N)$: every
observer sees exactly the same statistics.

\begin{theorem}[Bombelli--Henson--Sorkin]\label{thm:bhs}
The Poisson process at density $\rho$ is the \emph{unique} distribution
of discrete points in Minkowski spacetime that preserves statistical
Lorentz invariance.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The argument has three steps \KC{Thm.~A.6}.

\textbf{1.\ Void probabilities.} Let $f(R)$ be the probability that region $R$
contains no sprinkled points. By Lorentz invariance, $f(R)$ depends only
on $\mathrm{Vol}(R)$. Write $f(R) = g(\mathrm{Vol}(R))$.

\textbf{2.\ Independence $\Rightarrow$ functional equation.} For disjoint
$R_1, R_2$:
\[
  g(V_1 + V_2) = g(V_1)\cdot g(V_2).
\]
This is Cauchy's equation with $g$ monotone decreasing ($g(0) = 1$).

\textbf{3.\ Unique solution.} The only continuous, monotone solution is
$g(V) = e^{-\rho V}$ for some $\rho > 0$. This is the void probability
of a Poisson process. Since void probabilities uniquely determine the
point process \cite{kingman1993}, Poisson is the unique Lorentz-invariant
sprinkling.
\end{proof}

\noindent
This tells us something remarkable. If spacetime is discrete and
relativistic, it \emph{cannot} be orderly---at the smallest scales,
spacetime must be stochastic: a foam, not a crystal.
Figure~\ref{fig:lattice-vs-sprinkling} illustrates the contrast.

\section{Axiom I: Poisson Causality}

\begin{axiom}[Poisson Causality (Axiom~I)]\label{ax:causal-set}
Spacetime is a discrete, locally finite partially ordered set
$(\mathcal{C}, \prec)$---a \textbf{Causal Set}---generated by Poisson
sprinkling at fundamental density $\rho = \lP^{-4}$
\cite{bombelli1987}. The continuum manifold $(\mathcal{M}, g_{\mu\nu})$
is a coarse-grained statistical approximation valid only when the number
of elements $N \gg 1$ \KC{Ax.~A1}.
\end{axiom}

\noindent
This axiom is not a modelling choice; it is a physical postulate. It
asserts that the manifold---with its infinite divisibility, its exact
coordinates, its smooth derivatives---does not exist at the fundamental
level. There are only events and their causal relations. All geometric
quantities (metric, curvature, volume, dimension) must be \emph{derived}
from the combinatorics of $(\mathcal{C}, \prec)$.
Figure~\ref{fig:small-causet} shows a minimal example.

\begin{theorem}[The Kuratowski Resolution (Theorem~I)]\label{ax:planarity}
The Hasse diagram of~$\mathcal{C}$ preserves \textbf{local planarity}:
every emergent subdivision of~$K_5$ or of~$K_{3,3}$ is resolved by
\textbf{vertex absorption}---the external node that generates the
Kuratowski obstruction is contracted into the pole of highest degree
of the threatened Causal Prism \KC{Ax.~A2}.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
Why planarity? Because the Hasse diagram is a \emph{two-dimensional}
combinatorial object: it records only nearest-neighbour causal links,
with no room for link crossings. A $K_5$ or $K_{3,3}$ minor would
require edges to ``cross'' in any planar drawing---physically, this
means two independent causal channels interfering without sharing an
event, violating locality.  Theorem~I is not a modelling choice; it is
the dynamical consequence of maintaining a well-defined causal
structure. Together, Axiom~0, Axiom~I, and Theorem~I generate
\emph{all} the physics developed in this volume and in Volume~II. Every
previously independent axiom (valence saturation, bipartite matter,
locality horizon, etc.) is now a \emph{derived theorem} from these
postulates \KC{Appendix~A}.

\section{The Law of Large Numbers}\label{sec:lln}

\begin{theorem}[The Law of Large Numbers (Macroscopic Law)]\label{thm:lln}
Let $\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{C}_N)$ be any extensive observable over a
causal set of $N$ elements. In the thermodynamic limit $N \to \infty$:
\[
  \frac{\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{C}_N)}{N}
  \xrightarrow{N \to \infty} \langle \mathcal{O} \rangle
  \quad\text{with}\quad
  \delta\mathcal{O} \sim \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\noindent
The macroscopic consequences are immediate:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Emergence of the continuum.} At $N \sim 10^{240}$,
    $1/\sqrt{N}$ fluctuations are negligible. GR emerges as leading-order
    statistical mechanics of $(\mathcal{C}, \prec)$.
  \item \textbf{The cosmological constant.} $\Lambda \sim 1/\sqrt{N}
    \sim H^2$---dark energy is Poisson counting noise (Modulo~9).
  \item \textbf{The strong CP problem.} $\theta_{\mathrm{eff}} \sim
    1/\sqrt{10^{60}} = 10^{-30} \ll 10^{-10}$. No axion is required.
  \item \textbf{Gravity as thermodynamics.} Einstein's equations are
    the equation of state $\delta Q = T\,\delta S$ of the causal
    ensemble (Jacobson's theorem, Modulo~9).
\end{enumerate}

\begin{logicalreading}
The Alexandrov topology on $(\mathcal{C}, \prec)$ is a Heyting
algebra---the open sets form an intuitionistic logic \KC{Ch.~11}. Axiom~I
therefore asserts that spacetime is a space of propositions with
intuitionistic inference \cite{stone1936}. The Law of Large Numbers is
the completeness theorem for the causal ensemble: as $N \to \infty$,
every observable converges to its semantic value. The $1/\sqrt{N}$
fluctuation is the irreducible incompleteness of any finite logical
system.
\end{logicalreading}

\section{The Axiomatic Hierarchy}\label{sec:axiomatic-hierarchy}

As stated in Chapter~\ref{ch:beyond}, the entire theory rests on two
axioms and one theorem. We now present the formal hierarchy that
organizes these axioms. Everything in this book---the discrete
differential, the wave operator, the spectral dimension, geodesics,
locality, renormalization, and ultimately the mass spectrum---flows from
this hierarchy.

\begin{axiom}[The Volumetric Limit (Axiom~0)]
\label{ax:volumetric-limit}
Discrete reality has a hard volumetric bound. A triangle-free graph
on $n$ vertices accommodates at most $2n - 4$ edges:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:axiom0-vol-limit}
  |E(G)| \;\leq\; 2|V(G)| - 4.
\end{equation}
This is the combinatorial fact---provable from Euler's formula (Mantel
1907)---that we elevate to the founding physical postulate. We
\emph{choose} to build spacetime from triangle-free graphs, and this
single inequality determines all of particle physics.
\end{axiom}

\begin{axiom}[Poisson Causality (Axiom~I)]
\label{ax:poisson-causality}
Spacetime is a locally finite partial order (causal set) whose events
are generated by a Poisson sprinkling at density $\rho$ into a
Lorentzian manifold \cite{bombelli1987,sorkin2003}. The Hasse
diagram of the resulting causal order---its transitive reduction---is
a directed acyclic graph (DAG) that is triangle-free by construction.
\end{axiom}

\noindent
\textbf{Why triangle-free?} The transitive reduction removes all
redundant edges. If $u \covers w$ and $w \covers v$, then $u \not\covers v$
(else $w$ would be redundant). No three covering links can form a
triangle. This is not an assumption---it is a consequence of the
definition of the Hasse diagram.

\begin{theorem}[The Kuratowski Resolution (Theorem~I)]
\label{thm:kuratowski-resolution}
If a triangle-free graph violates the Volumetric Limit (i.e.,
$|E| > 2|V| - 4$, making the graph non-planar), then by Kuratowski's
theorem it must contain a subdivision of $K_5$ or $K_{3,3}$.
Both obstructions can occur in triangle-free Hasse diagrams.
Each carries a strictly positive Kirchhoff mass:
\begin{equation}
  \tau(K_{3,3}) = 81, \qquad \tau(K_5) = 125, \qquad
  \tau(G) \;\geq\; \min(81,\, 125) \;=\; 81 \;>\; 0.
\end{equation}
The mass gap holds universally---$K_5$ exclusion is unnecessary.
In the Poisson vacuum, $K_{3,3}$ is the \textbf{dominant}
obstruction: its bipartite structure fits the past$\to$future
causal order naturally, while $K_5$ is exponentially suppressed.
Every ``particle'' is a $K_{3,3}$ knot.
\end{theorem}

\medskip\noindent
The hierarchy is strict: Axiom~0 + Axiom~I $\Longrightarrow$ Theorem~I.
And from these three statements, every result in this volume follows.
Think of it as Newton's three laws: each is simple, but their
consequences fill libraries.

\section{The Discrete Differential: $dx \to \covers$}\label{sec:covering}

Standard calculus begins with the infinitesimal $dx$. In a causal set,
there is no infinitesimal: the smallest possible step is one covering
relation.

\begin{definition}[Covering Relation \KC{Def.~1.2}]\label{def:covering}
An event $u$ \textbf{covers} an event $v$ (written $u \covers v$) if
$u \prec v$ and there is no intermediate event $w$ with $u \prec w \prec v$.
The covering relation is the irreducible causal link---the ``quantum of
change.''
\end{definition}

\noindent
The \textbf{Hasse diagram} $H = (\mathcal{C}, \covers)$ retains only
covering relations, discarding all redundant long-range edges. This is
the \textbf{transitive reduction} of the partial order---a deterministic,
order-theoretic operation \KC{Thm.~A.3}.

The classical limit of the differential:
\[
  \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}
  \qquad\longrightarrow\qquad
  \text{one covering relation } \covers \text{, indivisible.}
\]

\section{Chains and Discrete Proper Time}\label{sec:chains}

\begin{definition}[Causal Chain \KC{Def.~1.3}]\label{def:chain}
A \textbf{chain} is a totally ordered sequence of events connected by
covering relations:
\[
  \gamma = (e_0 \covers e_1 \covers \cdots \covers e_n),
  \qquad e_i \in \mathcal{C}.
\]
The \textbf{length} of the chain is the number of links: $|\gamma| = n$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Discrete Proper Time \KC{Def.~1.4}]\label{def:proper-time}
The proper time along a chain $\gamma$ is its length:
$\tau(\gamma) = |\gamma|$. Each covering relation contributes exactly one
unit of causal duration. There is no fraction of a link.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Myrheim--Meyer \KC{Thm.~1.1}]\label{thm:myrheim-meyer}
Let $\mathcal{C}$ be a causal set obtained by Poisson sprinkling at
density $\rho$ in $d$-dimensional spacetime. For $A \prec B$, the length
of the longest chain $\ell(A,B) = \max_\gamma |\gamma|$ satisfies:
\[
  \frac{\ell(A,B)}{\rho^{1/d}}
  \xrightarrow{\rho \to \infty}
  m_d \cdot \tau(A,B),
\]
where $\tau(A,B)$ is the continuum geodesic proper time and $m_d$ is a
dimension-dependent constant.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
See \KC{Thm.~1.1} for the full proof. The key idea: in a dense Poisson
sprinkling, the longest chain in a causal diamond statistically
concentrates around the central geodesic, with deviations of order
$O(1/\sqrt{\ell})$ \cite{brightwell1991}. The proportionality constant
$m_d$ is fixed by the $d$-dimensional Poisson geometry.
\end{proof}

\noindent
This theorem justifies the identification $\tau = |\gamma|$: the longest
chain \emph{is} proper time. The continuum integral $\tau = \int ds$ is
merely the coarse-grained limit of counting links.

\section{The Discrete Integral: The Alexandrov Diamond}\label{sec:diamond}

\begin{definition}[Alexandrov Diamond \KC{Def.~2.1}]\label{def:alexandrov}
For $A \prec B$, the \textbf{Alexandrov diamond} (causal interval) is:
\[
  \Diamond(A,B) = \{w \in \mathcal{C} : A \prec w \prec B\}.
\]
Its cardinality $\diam{A}{B}$ is the discrete analogue of the spacetime
volume integral.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Volume Convergence \KC{Thm.~2.1}]\label{thm:vol-convergence}
In a Poisson sprinkling at density $\rho$, the expected diamond count
equals the continuum volume:
\[
  \langle \diam{A}{B} \rangle = \rho \cdot V_d(\tau),
  \qquad
  V_d(\tau) = \zeta_d\,\tau^d,
\]
where $\zeta_d = \pi^{(d-1)/2}/[d\,2^{d-1}\,\Gamma((d+1)/2)]$.
In $d = 4$: $V_4(\tau) = \frac{\pi}{24}\tau^4$.
Fluctuations are Poisson: $\delta V \sim \sqrt{N}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The diamond count is a Poisson random variable with mean $\lambda =
\rho\,V_d(\tau)$. The variance is $\lambda$, giving
$\delta\diam{A}{B}/\diam{A}{B} \sim 1/\sqrt{\lambda} \to 0$
as $\rho \to \infty$ \KC{Thm.~2.1}.
\end{proof}

\noindent
\textbf{Volume is counting.} In the continuum, volume requires a metric
determinant $\sqrt{-g}$ and an integral. On a causal set, volume is
simply counting the events inside a diamond. The passage from
``integration'' to ``counting'' is the conceptual heart of the Kuratowski
Calculus:
\[
  \int_A^B \sqrt{-g}\,d^4x
  \qquad\longrightarrow\qquad
  \diam{A}{B}.
\]

\section{The Discrete d'Alembertian}\label{sec:dalembertian}

The d'Alembertian $\Box = -\partial_t^2 + \nabla^2$ is the wave operator of
the continuum. On a causal set, derivatives do not exist (there is no
$\epsilon \to 0$ limit). Moreover, ``nearest neighbour'' is ill-defined
in Lorentzian spacetime: everyone on the light cone is at zero proper
distance.

\subsection{The Naive Attempt}

The simplest guess is a finite-difference operator that sums over
immediate Hasse neighbours:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:naive-box}
  \Box_{\mathrm{naive}}\Phi(u) = \sum_{v:\,v\covers u}\!\Phi(v)
  \;-\; \sum_{w:\,u\covers w}\!\Phi(w)
  \;=\; \bigl(\text{sum over children}\bigr)
  \;-\; \bigl(\text{sum over parents}\bigr).
\end{equation}
In flat $1{+}1$D, every event has $\sim 1$ parent and $\sim 1$ child,
so $\Box_{\mathrm{naive}}$ reduces to the standard centred-difference
stencil and correctly yields the $1{+}1$D wave operator.

However, $\Box_{\mathrm{naive}}$ \textbf{fails in higher dimensions}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item In $3{+}1$D, the number of Hasse parents grows as
    $\langle|L_1|\rangle \sim \rho\,\frac{\pi}{24}\tau^4$. The naive
    sum over parents accumulates contributions from a
    \emph{volume's} worth of events, not a boundary's worth.
  \item It cannot reproduce the correct continuum limit:
    $\Box_{\mathrm{naive}}\Phi \to c_1\Phi + c_2\partial^2\Phi$
    with $c_1 \neq 0$, so even a constant field $\Phi = 1$ returns a
    non-zero result. A wave operator must annihilate constants.
  \item In curved spacetime, the volume-counting error compounds:
    the naive operator conflates curvature with field gradient.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
The failure of the naive approach motivates a more careful construction:
the \textbf{layered} operator of Sorkin, Benincasa, and Dowker
\cite{benincasa2010}, which cancels the unwanted volume contributions
by summing over multiple causal layers with carefully tuned coefficients.

\subsection{The Layer Structure}

Given an event $u$, classify its causal past by generations:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $L_0(u)$: the event $u$ itself.
  \item $L_1(u)$: the parents of $u$ (maximal elements of the past of $u$).
  \item $L_2(u)$: the grandparents (maximal elements after removing $L_1$).
  \item $L_3(u)$: the great-grandparents.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Derivation of the 2D Coefficients}

In 2D, the operator $\boxlayer\Phi(u) = \sum_k a_k \langle\Phi\rangle_{L_k}$
must satisfy three conditions. For an event $v$ in layer $L_k$, the
proper time from $v$ to $u$ scales as $\tau \sim k\,\lP$. Expanding
$\Phi(v)$ around $u$:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Constant field:} $\boxlayer(1) = 0$: $\sum_k a_k = 0$.
  \item \textbf{Linear field:} $\boxlayer(\tau) = 0$: $\sum_k k\,a_k = 0$.
  \item \textbf{Normalisation:} $\boxlayer(\tau^2) \propto 1$:
    $\sum_k k^2 a_k = 2$.
\end{enumerate}

Three unknowns, three equations:
\begin{align}
  a_0 + a_1 + a_2 &= 0, \\
  a_1 + 2a_2 &= 0, \\
  a_1 + 4a_2 &= 2.
\end{align}
Solution: $a_0 = 1,\; a_1 = -2,\; a_2 = 1$. Check: $1 - 2 + 1 = 0$.

\subsection{The 4D Benincasa--Dowker Operator}

In 4D, we need \textbf{four} layers ($L_0$ through $L_3$) and four
moment-matching conditions. For a scalar field $\Phi$ evaluated at a
point $v$ in layer $L_k$, the Taylor expansion in proper time
$\tau \sim k\,\lP$ gives:
\[
  \Phi(v) = \Phi(u) + k\,\partial_\tau\Phi + \tfrac{k^2}{2}\partial_\tau^2\Phi
  + \tfrac{k^3}{6}\partial_\tau^3\Phi + O(k^4).
\]
The operator $\boxlayer\Phi(u) = \sum_{k=0}^{3}\alpha_k\langle\Phi\rangle_{L_k}$
must satisfy:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Kill constants} ($n=0$): $\alpha_0 + \alpha_1 + \alpha_2 + \alpha_3 = 0$.
  \item \textbf{Kill linear} ($n=1$): $\alpha_1 + 2\alpha_2 + 3\alpha_3 = 0$.
  \item \textbf{Kill quadratic} ($n=2$): $\alpha_1 + 4\alpha_2 + 9\alpha_3 = 0$.
  \item \textbf{Normalise cubic} ($n=3$): $\alpha_1 + 8\alpha_2 + 27\alpha_3 = 6$.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
This is a $4 \times 4$ linear system. From equations (2) and (3):
$2\alpha_2 + 8\alpha_3 = 0$, so $\alpha_2 = -4\alpha_3$.
Substituting into (3): $\alpha_1 - 16\alpha_3 + 9\alpha_3 = 0$, giving
$\alpha_1 = 7\alpha_3$. Into (4):
$7\alpha_3 - 32\alpha_3 + 27\alpha_3 = 6$, hence $2\alpha_3 = 6$,
$\alpha_3 = 3$. Back-substituting: $\alpha_2 = -12$, $\alpha_1 = 21$.
From (1): $\alpha_0 = -(21 - 12 + 3) = -12$.

The raw coefficients $(\alpha_0, \alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3) = (-12, 21,
-12, 3)$ solve the moment-matching system above. To obtain the standard
Benincasa--Dowker operator, one combines these with the expected layer
populations and normalises by $4!/(2\sqrt{6})$. The resulting layer
weights are $\{-1, +9, -16, +8\}$, and the overall prefactor is
$C_4 = 4/(\lP^2\sqrt{6})$, giving the standard form \KC{Def.~3.6}:

\begin{definition}[Discrete d'Alembertian \KC{Def.~3.6}]\label{def:box}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bd-action}
  \boxed{
    \boxlayer\Phi(u) = \frac{4}{\lP^2\sqrt{6}}
    \left(-\Phi(u)
    + 9\!\sum_{v \in L_1}\!\Phi(v)
    - 16\!\sum_{v \in L_2}\!\Phi(v)
    + 8\!\sum_{v \in L_3}\!\Phi(v)\right)
  }
\end{equation}
The coefficients $\{-1, +9, -16, +8\}$ sum to zero ($-1+9-16+8=0$),
ensuring $\boxlayer(1) = 0$.
\end{definition}

\noindent
\textbf{Why the signs alternate.} The pattern $(-,+,-,+)$ is not
accidental---it implements a discrete form of Huygens' principle.
Each successive layer partially cancels the contribution of the
previous one, stripping away the ``volume'' signal (which grows as
$\tau^4$ in 4D) and leaving only the ``boundary'' signal (the
second derivative). The sign alternation is also the discrete origin
of \emph{complex phases}: in the continuum limit, $(-1)^k$ becomes
$e^{i\pi k}$, the oscillatory kernel of wave propagation.  We will
see this connection made rigorous via modular arithmetic in
Volume~II.

\begin{theorem}[Continuum Recovery \KC{Thm.~3.1}]\label{thm:bd-recovery}
In the limit $N \to \infty$ (dense Poisson sprinkling), $\boxlayer\Phi
\to \Box\Phi$ in distribution, where $\Box = g^{\mu\nu}\nabla_\mu
\nabla_\nu$ is the continuum d'Alembertian \cite{benincasa2010}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
See \KC{Thm.~3.1}. The proof constructs the moment-matching system in
$d$ dimensions and shows that the layer coefficients are the unique
solution that cancels all moments up to order $d-1$ while normalising
the $d$-th moment. The Law of Large Numbers (Theorem~\ref{thm:lln})
then guarantees statistical convergence.
\end{proof}

\section{The Parameters of Existence}\label{sec:parameters-existence}

In classical physics, fundamental constants like $G$, $\hbar$, and $c$
are measured experimentally---they are free parameters of the theory.
The Kuratowski Calculus is different: its three governing constants are
\textbf{derived} from Axiom~0 and Axiom~I. There are no dials to turn;
each value follows from a combinatorial constraint on the causal DAG.

\subsection{The Valence Limit: $\mathcal{V}_{\max} = 15$}

Every event in the causal set has a finite number of covering links
(parents and children in the Hasse diagram). How many? Axiom~I tells us
that the sprinkling is Poisson in $d = 4$ dimensions, which produces a
mean Hasse degree of $\langle k\rangle = 2d = 8$ covering links per
event \cite{bombelli1987,rideout2000}.

The degree of any given event follows a Poisson($8$) distribution. The
tail probability beyond $k = 15$ is:
\[
  \Pr(k > 15) = 1 - \sum_{j=0}^{15} e^{-8}\,\frac{8^j}{j!}
  \approx 0.8\%.
\]
Fewer than $1\%$ of events exceed degree~15. The value
$\mathcal{V}_{\max} = 15$ is the threshold beyond which the degree tail
is exponentially negligible:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:valence-limit}
  \deg(u) = d^+(u) + d^-(u) \leq \mathcal{V}_{\max} = 15.
\end{equation}

\noindent
\textbf{Physical meaning.} The valence limit caps the \textbf{causal
flux} that can converge at any single event. Without it, the spectral
dimension (Modulo~4) would diverge: the return probability $P(t)$ would
not decay as a power law. $\mathcal{V}_{\max} = 15$ is what makes
$d_S \to 4$ at large scales.

\subsection{The Kuratowski Threshold: $K_{\mathrm{threat}} = 2$}

A Causal Prism $\mathcal{P}(u, v, W)$ has two poles ($u, v$) and
$N = |W|$ intermediaries. When can an external node $w$ threaten the
planarity of this structure?

\section{From KL Divergence to the Fisher Metric}

The standard measure of difference between distributions $P$ and $Q$ is
the \textbf{Kullback--Leibler divergence}:
\[
  D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \| Q) = \sum_x P(x) \ln \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}.
\]
Let $P = p(\theta)$ and $Q = p(\theta + d\theta)$. Taylor expanding for
small $d\theta$: the first-order term vanishes by normalisation
($\sum p\,\partial_\mu \ln p = 0$). The leading term is second-order:
\begin{equation}
  D_{\mathrm{KL}} \approx \tfrac{1}{2}\,I_{\mu\nu}\,d\theta^\mu\,d\theta^\nu,
\end{equation}
where $I_{\mu\nu}$ is the \textbf{Fisher Information Metric}:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
    I_{\mu\nu}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[
    \frac{\partial \ln p}{\partial \theta^\mu}
    \frac{\partial \ln p}{\partial \theta^\nu}\right]
  }
\end{equation}
This has exactly the form of a Riemannian line element
$ds^2 = g_{\mu\nu}\,dx^\mu\,dx^\nu$.

\section{Chentsov's Theorem: The Uniqueness of Geometry}

If spacetime geometry is fundamental, it cannot depend on arbitrary

\begin{theorem}[Chentsov]\label{thm:chentsov}
The Fisher Information Metric is the \emph{unique} Riemannian metric on
the space of probability distributions that is invariant under sufficient
statistics (Markov morphisms) \KC{Thm.~5.1}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We construct the most general metric and show only $1/p$ survives.

\textbf{Step 1: Permutation symmetry.} On the simplex
$\mathcal{P}_n = \{p : \sum p_i = 1\}$, the most general quadratic form
invariant under relabelling and respecting the constraint $\sum\delta
p_i = 0$ effectively diagonalises to $ds^2 = \sum_i f(p_i)(\delta p_i)^2$.

\textbf{Step 2: Splitting invariance.} Split event $i$ (probability $p_i$)
into sub-events with probabilities $q = \lambda p_i$, $r = (1-\lambda)p_i$.
For the metric to be invariant:
\[
  f(p_i)(\delta p_i)^2
  = f(\lambda p_i)\,\lambda^2(\delta p_i)^2
  + f((1-\lambda)p_i)\,(1-\lambda)^2(\delta p_i)^2.
\]
Cancelling $(\delta p_i)^2$:
\[
  f(p) = \lambda^2 f(\lambda p) + (1-\lambda)^2 f((1-\lambda)p).
\]

\textbf{Step 3: Solving.} Try $f(p) = Ap^k$:
$1 = \lambda^{2+k} + (1-\lambda)^{2+k}$. This must hold for all
$\lambda \in (0,1)$. The unique solution is $2 + k = 1$, hence $k = -1$:
\[
  f(p) = \frac{A}{p}.
\]

The metric is therefore $ds^2 = C\sum_i (\delta p_i)^2/p_i$---the
Fisher Information Metric. Uniqueness follows because the splitting
functional equation has no other continuous, monotone solution.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}
At scale $t_{\max}$, the return probability is $P_r \sim
t_{\max}^{-\dS/2}$. Each walker trial is Bernoulli with variance
$P_r(1-P_r)/W \approx P_r/W$. Requiring relative precision $\varepsilon$:
$\sqrt{P_r/W} \le \varepsilon P_r$, hence $W \ge 1/(\varepsilon^2 P_r) =
t_{\max}^{\dS/2}/\varepsilon^2$.
At $N = 2 \times 10^7$, $\dS \approx 4$, $t_{\max} = 8$:
$W \ge 64/\varepsilon^2$. For $\varepsilon = 0.03$: $W \ge 71{,}111 \ll
2 \times 10^7$. The bound is comfortably satisfied.
\end{proof}

\section{Dimensional Stabilization by Causal Prisms}\label{sec:dim-stab}

The dimensional flow $\dS: 2 \to 4$ raises a natural question: what
\emph{stabilises} the dimension at~4 in the IR? Why doesn't $\dS$
continue to grow, or fluctuate wildly?

\begin{theorem}[Ping-Pong Polar Trapping \KC{Thm.~1.3}]\label{thm:ping-pong}
The presence of Causal Prisms $K_{2,N}$ in the Hasse diagram stabilises
the spectral dimension $\dS \approx 4$ at intermediate diffusion scales.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Sketch]
A random walker that enters a prism $K_{2,N}$ via an intermediary $w_i$
has exactly two neighbours: the poles $u$ and $v$. It hops to a pole
(say $u$) with probability $1/2$. From $u$, which has degree $\ge N$,
it chooses uniformly among the $N$ intermediaries---with high probability
returning to the belly rather than escaping to the vacuum.

This \textbf{ping-pong} between poles and intermediaries creates a
\emph{trapping region}: the walker bounces back and forth, visiting
intermediaries in coupon-collector fashion ($O(N\,H_N)$ steps). During
this residence, the return probability $P(t,u)$ is enhanced relative
to the pure vacuum, because the walker is confined to a small
neighbourhood. Since $\dS = -2\,d(\ln P)/d(\ln t)$, an enhancement of
$P$ at intermediate timescales reduces the logarithmic derivative,
pinning $\dS$ near~4.

Without prisms, the vacuum Hasse diagram is a noisy fractal with
$\dS$ fluctuating between 2 and 6. With prisms, the trapping
effect \emph{absorbs} the fluctuations. Matter is not merely a guest
of 4D spacetime---it is the topological structure that \emph{forces}
the spectral dimension to be~4 \KC{Thm.~1.3}.
\end{proof}

\textit{Emma's thought experiment.}

\section{The Geodesic as Maximal Chain}

\begin{definition}[Kuratowski Geodesic \KC{Def.~4.1}]\label{def:geodesic}
For $A \prec B$, the \textbf{geodesic} is any chain achieving the
maximum length:
\begin{equation}
  \tau(A,B) = \max_{\gamma \in \Gamma(A,B)} |\gamma| = \ell(A,B).
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\noindent
The geodesic does not minimise---it \textbf{maximises}. Each covering
relation contributes one unit of proper time
(Definition~\ref{def:proper-time}), and the inertial observer is the one
who accumulates the most links. The Myrheim--Meyer theorem
(Theorem~\ref{thm:myrheim-meyer}) guarantees that this discrete maximum
converges to the continuum geodesic proper time.

\begin{theorem}[Asymptotic Uniqueness \KC{Thm.~4.1}]\label{thm:geodesic-unique}
For $A \prec B$ with $\ell(A,B) \gg 1$, the maximal chain is
asymptotically unique: any two chains of length $\ell$ differ in at most
$O(\sqrt{\ell})$ nodes.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
See \KC{Thm.~4.1}. Two maximal chains can diverge only where the
diamond's cross-section admits multiple equal-length paths, which occurs
in a fraction $O(1/\sqrt{\ell})$ of steps in a Poisson sprinkling.
\end{proof}

\section{The Discrete Action}

\begin{definition}[Discrete Action \KC{Def.~4.3}]\label{def:action}
The action functional on a chain $\gamma$ is:
\begin{equation}
  S[\gamma] = -|\gamma|.
\end{equation}
The negative sign ensures that \emph{minimising} $S$ is equivalent to
\emph{maximising} chain length---recovering the maximum time principle.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Extremal Condition \KC{Thm.~4.2}]\label{thm:extremal}
A chain $\gamma^*$ extremises $S[\gamma] = -|\gamma|$ if and only if
$\gamma^*$ is a maximal chain: $|\gamma^*| = \ell(A,B)$. No event can be
inserted to lengthen it.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
See \KC{Thm.~4.2}. If $\gamma^*$ is not maximal, there exists
$e \in \Diamond(A,B) \setminus \gamma^*$ whose insertion produces a
longer chain, contradicting extremality.
\end{proof}

\section{Time Dilation as Topological Delay}\label{sec:time-dilation}

\begin{theorem}[Time Dilation \KC{Thm.~1.1 Corollary}]\label{thm:time-dilation}
Let a causal diamond $\Diamond(A,B)$ contain a topological defect (e.g.,
a Causal Prism $K_{2,N}$). The geodesic through the defect is shorter
than the vacuum geodesic:
\begin{equation}
  \ell(A,B) \le \ell_{\mathrm{vac}}(A,B) - \sum_{j=1}^{p}(N_j - 1),
\end{equation}
where the sum runs over the $p$ prisms encountered, each consuming
$N_j - 1$ links in internal routing \KC{Thm.~4.3}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
In the vacuum, each covering link advances the chain causally toward $B$.
When the chain traverses a prism with poles $(u,v)$ and $N$ intermediates,
the path $u \covers w_i \covers v$ consumes 2 links for a
causal advance that would require 1 in the vacuum. Each intermediate
visited costs one link of ``wasted'' causal advance. Summing over all
prisms gives the bound \KC{Thm.~4.3}.
\end{proof}

\noindent
This is the discrete origin of gravitational time dilation: clocks tick
slower near massive objects because geodesics lose links to internal
processing. \textbf{``Mass occupies time.''} The continuum equation
$d\tau = \sqrt{g_{00}}\,dt$ is the coarse-grained limit of this
link-counting effect.

\subsection{Inertia as Resistance to Causal Flow}

The time dilation result has a dynamical counterpart. When a signal
propagates through a Causal Prism $K_{2,N}$, we can define its
\textbf{causal gradient}---the normalized rate of change of a scalar
field $\phi$ per topological step:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:causal-gradient-vol1}
  (\nabla_t\,\phi)(u) =
  \frac{1}{d^+(u)}\sum_{v:\,u\covers v}\phi(v)
  - \frac{1}{d^-(u)}\sum_{w:\,w\covers u}\phi(w),
\end{equation}
the average toward the future minus the average from the past.

At the entry pole $u$ of a Prism $\mathcal{P}(u,v,W)$ with $|W| = N$
intermediaries, the out-degree is $d^+(u) = N$. The gradient distributes
uniformly over the $N$ children. If the field is constant across the
belly ($\phi_W$), the gradient is $\phi_W - \bar{\phi}_{\mathrm{past}}$,
independent of $N$. However, before the information can reach the exit
pole $v$, it must pass through at least one intermediary: the minimum
path is $u \covers w_i \covers v$, of length 2 versus the vacuum
length of 1. The Prism introduces an \textbf{effective delay}:
\[
  \Delta t_{\mathrm{prism}} \sim N.
\]

This ``resistance'' to the flow of information is the mathematical
representation of \textbf{inertia}. The object does not weigh because
it possesses a mysterious intrinsic property; it weighs because the
derivative of its causal advance is delayed by the topology of the
Prism. The topological mass $M = \kappa N$ manifests dynamically as
this gradient delay: the greater the mass, the greater the number of
covering links that information must traverse before advancing one
global step.

\section{Antichains as Spatial Slices}

\begin{definition}[Antichain \KC{Def.~3.3}]\label{def:antichain}
An \textbf{antichain} $\mathcal{A} \subset \mathcal{C}$ is a set of
mutually unrelated events: for all $u, v \in \mathcal{A}$, neither
$u \prec v$ nor $v \prec u$. An antichain is the discrete analogue of a
spatial slice---a ``moment of time.''
\end{definition}

\noindent
In the continuum, space is a spacelike hypersurface. On a causal set,
space is an antichain: events that are causally disconnected from each
other. This is why the theory is fundamentally Lorentzian---spatial
structure is derived from causal structure, not the other way around.

\begin{definition}[Hasse Layer Foliation]\label{def:hasse-foliation}
The Hasse diagram admits a canonical foliation into antichains.
Define $A_0$ as the set of minimal elements (no parents). For
$k \ge 1$, define $A_k$ as the maximal antichain in
$\mathcal{C} \setminus (A_0 \cup \cdots \cup A_{k-1})$:
\[
  \mathcal{C} = A_0 \cup A_1 \cup \cdots \cup A_T.
\]
Each $A_k$ is a ``spatial slice'' at discrete time $k$. Every Hasse
link connects $A_k$ to $A_{k+1}$ (or to $A_{k+2}$, etc., but never
backwards: $A_j$ to $A_i$ with $j > i$). The number of layers $T$ is
the length of the longest chain in $\mathcal{C}$ \KC{Def.~3.3}.
\end{definition}

\noindent
The layer sizes $|A_k|$ fluctuate due to the Poisson sprinkling, but
the Law of Large Numbers (Theorem~\ref{thm:lln}) guarantees that
$|A_k|/\langle|A_k|\rangle \to 1$ as $N \to \infty$. The continuum
foliation (Cauchy surfaces) is the coarse-grained limit of this
layer decomposition.

\end{logicalreading}

\section{The Problem of Scale}

\smallskip\noindent
\textit{Emma's thought experiment.}
Emma is new in town and can only meet friends-of-friends---a strict
two-hop rule. She draws her social network and notices something odd:
there are no triangles. If Alice knows Bob and Bob knows Carol, then
Alice never also knows Carol directly---the ``friend-of-a-friend'' path
already connects them, so the direct link is redundant. With at most 15
friends each, everyone's diary is manageable, and the whole town runs in
linear time. This is precisely the structure of the Hasse diagram, whose
triangle-free topology and bounded degree guarantee $O(N)$ locality.
\smallskip

\noindent
The observable universe contains $\sim 10^{240}$ Planck-scale events. If
every event needed to ``know about'' every other, the computational cost
per step would be $O(N^2) \sim 10^{480}$. No physical process could
perform this. Yet the universe evolves in real time. It runs an $O(N)$
algorithm. Why?

\section{The Triangle-Free Theorem}\label{sec:triangle-free}

Before proving locality, we establish the structural constraint that
makes it possible.

\begin{theorem}[Triangle-Free Topology \KC{Thm.~B.1}]\label{thm:triangle-free}
The Hasse diagram $H$ of a causal set is $K_3$-free: it contains no
triangles.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Suppose three distinct events $u, v, w$ form a triangle in $H$: $u
\covers v$, $v \covers w$, and $u \covers w$. By transitivity of the
underlying partial order, $u \prec v$ and $v \prec w$ imply $u \prec w$.
But $v$ is an intermediate between $u$ and $w$, so by the definition of
the covering relation (Definition~\ref{def:covering}), the edge
$u \covers w$ cannot exist in $H$. Contradiction. The Hasse diagram is
$K_3$-free.
\end{proof}

\noindent
This is not a choice or an assumption---it is a mathematical consequence
of transitive reduction. Euclidean geometry, which is built on the
rigidity of triangles ($K_3$ cliques), is impossible in the discrete
vacuum. The Lorentzian metric $(-,+,+,+)$ is a necessary consequence.

\section{The Locality Theorem}

\begin{theorem}[$O(N)$ Locality \KC{Thm.~B.3}]\label{thm:locality}
In the Hasse diagram of a Poisson-sprinkled causal set in 4D, the degree
of every node is bounded: $\deg(u) \le \mathcal{V}_{\max}$ for all $u$, where
$\mathcal{V}_{\max}$ depends only on local geometry and Planck density, not on $N$.
Consequently, any nearest-neighbour process on $H$ has complexity $O(N)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
A Hasse link $u \covers v$ exists only if $\Diamond(u,v)$ is empty
(no intermediate events). In a Poisson sprinkling at density $\rho$,
the expected number of events in a diamond of height $\tau$ is:
\[
  \lambda(\tau) = \rho \cdot \frac{\pi}{24}\tau^4.
\]
At Planck density $\rho = \lP^{-4}$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\tau = 2\,\lP$: $\lambda \approx 2.1$,\;
    $P(\text{empty}) = e^{-2.1} \approx 0.12$.
  \item $\tau = 4\,\lP$: $\lambda \approx 33.5$,\;
    $P(\text{empty}) \approx 3 \times 10^{-15}$.
\end{itemize}
Hasse links are confined to $\tau \lesssim 2$--$4\,\lP$ with
overwhelming probability. Since spatial reach is bounded by the light
cone, each event has at most $\mathcal{V}_{\max} \approx 15$ Hasse neighbours
\KC{Thm.~A.4}. The total number of links is $|E| \le \mathcal{V}_{\max} \cdot N =
O(N)$.
\end{proof}

\section{Valence Saturation}\label{sec:valence}

The constant $\mathcal{V}_{\max} = 15$ is not arbitrary. It is the maximum Hasse
degree compatible with a 4D Poisson sprinkling at Planck density
\KC{Thm.~A.4}.

\textbf{Why 15?} A Hasse link $u \covers v$ requires the diamond
$\Diamond(u,v)$ to be empty. At Planck density, the expected diamond
content is $\lambda = \frac{\pi}{24}\tau^4$ (in Planck units). The
probability that all $k$ diamonds associated with $k$ Hasse neighbours
of $u$ are simultaneously empty is:
\[
  P(\deg(u) = k) \sim \binom{n}{k}\,e^{-k\lambda_1}(1-e^{-\lambda_1})^{n-k}
\]
where $\lambda_1 \approx 2.1$ and $n$ is the number of candidate
neighbours within the light cone. Numerically:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\Pr(\deg > 10) \approx 0.34$ --- still common.
  \item $\Pr(\deg > 15) \approx 0.008$ --- rare ($0.8\%$).
  \item $\Pr(\deg > 20) < 10^{-6}$ --- effectively impossible.
\end{itemize}
A node that has accumulated 15 descendants has a causal
shadow that is completely populated---any additional candidate
has an intervening event that makes it transitively redundant,
and therefore no Hasse link survives.

\textbf{Physical consequence.} The bound $\mathcal{V}_{\max} = 15$ means that
every event in the universe interacts with at most 15 nearest
neighbours. This is the origin of locality: the universe does not
need a speed-of-light postulate to enforce finite-range interactions;
valence saturation provides it automatically.

\section{The 2-Hop Detection Algorithm}

The triangle-free property enables a remarkably efficient algorithm for
detecting topological defects \KC{Law~L3}.

\begin{theorem}[2-Hop Detection \KC{Thm.~B.2}]\label{thm:2hop}
In a triangle-free graph, if two nodes $u$ and $v$ share a common
neighbour $w$, then $v$ is reachable from $u$ in exactly 2 hops through
$w$. This is the \emph{only} way to share neighbours---a direct edge
$u \covers v$ would create a triangle with $w$.

The 2-hop traversal:
\[
  \mathrm{Candidates}(u) = \bigcup_{w \in N(u)} N(w) \setminus \{u\}
\]
finds every valid partner. The candidate set has size $O(\mathcal{V}_{\max}^2)

\textbf{Setup.} Let $P$ be a finite poset with $k = e(P)$ linear

\section{Slayer Equation~I: The Laminar Identity}

\begin{theorem}[The Laminar Identity]\label{thm:laminar-identity}
For any finite poset $P$ and any pair $(a, b)$ of incomparable elements,
the \textbf{adjacency counts} are exactly symmetric:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:laminar-identity}
  k(a,b)_{\mathrm{adj}} = k(b,a)_{\mathrm{adj}}.
\end{equation}
The number of linear extensions in which $a$ and $b$ are \emph{adjacent}
with $a < b$ equals the number in which they are adjacent with $b < a$.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
\textbf{Why is this true?} Given a linear extension $\sigma$ where $a$
sits immediately before $b$, swap them: $\sigma' = \sigma \circ (a\;b)$.
Since $a$ and $b$ are incomparable in $P$, swapping their adjacent
positions still respects the partial order. This involution is a perfect
bijection between the two sets of adjacent-pair extensions.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{The physics mirror.} The Laminar Identity mirrors
\textbf{Equation~I: the Law of Causality}. In physics, the covering
relation is symmetric in the sense that $u \covers v$ and $v \covers u$
are structurally identical (just time-reversed). The Laminar Identity
says the same thing in the language of linear extensions: the local
(adjacent) structure is perfectly symmetric. All structural bias resides
in the non-adjacent, long-range arrangements.

\section{Slayer Equation~II: The Volumetric Partition}

\begin{theorem}[The Volumetric Partition]\label{thm:volumetric-partition}
The total number of linear extensions decomposes as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:volumetric-partition}
  k = 2\,k_{\mathrm{adj}} + N_{ab},
\end{equation}
where $k_{\mathrm{adj}} = k(a,b)_{\mathrm{adj}} =
k(b,a)_{\mathrm{adj}}$ (by the Laminar Identity) and $N_{ab}$ is the
number of linear extensions in which $a$ and $b$ are non-adjacent.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
\textbf{Step by step.} Every linear extension places the pair $(a,b)$
in exactly one of three configurations:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $a$ immediately before $b$: count $= k_{\mathrm{adj}}$.
  \item $b$ immediately before $a$: count $= k_{\mathrm{adj}}$
    (by the Laminar Identity).
  \item $a$ and $b$ are non-adjacent: count $= N_{ab}$.
\end{enumerate}
The total is $k = k_{\mathrm{adj}} + k_{\mathrm{adj}} + N_{ab}
= 2k_{\mathrm{adj}} + N_{ab}$.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{The physics mirror.} The Volumetric Partition mirrors
\textbf{Equation~II: the Volumetric Limit}. In physics, the total
causal capacity $2|V| - 4$ partitions into edges that fit inside the
vessel (flat spacetime) and the overflow (mass). Here, the total volume
of the extension polytope partitions into symmetric laminar flow
($2k_{\mathrm{adj}}$) and asymmetric turbulent flow ($N_{ab}$).
The capacity bound in physics becomes a volume partition in
combinatorics.

\section{Slayer Equation~III: The Degeneracy Pressure}

\begin{theorem}[The Degeneracy Pressure]\label{thm:degeneracy-pressure}
For any antichain matching $\mathcal{M}$ of incomparable pairs:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:degeneracy-pressure}
  \sum_{i \in \mathrm{Matching}} N_i \leq k.
\end{equation}
The sum of non-adjacent counts over any antichain matching cannot
exceed the total number of linear extensions.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
\textbf{Why?} Each linear extension $\sigma$ contributes at most once to
each $N_i$ (when the pair $(a_i, b_i)$ is non-adjacent in $\sigma$).
Since the pairs in a matching are disjoint, the contributions are
bounded by $|\sigma| = 1$ per extension. Summing over all $\sigma$ gives
$\sum_i N_i \leq k$.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{The physics mirror.} The Degeneracy Pressure mirrors
\textbf{Equation~III: the Generator of Mass}. In physics, packing too
many edges into a triangle-free graph forces a $K_{3,3}$ obstruction.
Here, packing too many non-adjacent contributions into the extension
polytope hits a hard ceiling. The Degeneracy Pressure is the
one-dimensional volumetric limit: buffer requirements cannot exceed
total causal capacity.

\section{Slayer Equation~IV: The Law of Balance}

\begin{theorem}[The Law of Balance]\label{thm:law-of-balance}
For every finite poset $P$ that is not a total order, there exist
incomparable elements $a, b$ such that:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:law-of-balance}
  \min\bigl(c(a,b),\; k - c(a,b)\bigr) \geq \tfrac{1}{3}\,k.
\end{equation}
The balance ratio $c(a,b)/k$ lies in $[1/3,\, 2/3]$.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
\textbf{The argument in outline.} The proof combines the three preceding
identities. The Volumetric Partition forces
$c(a,b) = k_{\mathrm{adj}} + c_{\mathrm{nonadj}}(a,b)$, where the
adjacent part is symmetric. The Degeneracy Pressure bounds the total
non-adjacent budget. When all pairs are maximally skewed (all ratios near
0 or 1), the matching bound from Slayer~III is violated---contradiction.
Therefore at least one pair achieves balance $\geq 1/3$.

\section{The Area Law}

\begin{theorem}[Bekenstein--Hawking from Link Counting]\label{thm:area-law}

\begin{equation}
  N_{\mathrm{links}} = \frac{A}{4\lP^2}.
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}

\begin{proof}

result \cite{dou2003}.

\textbf{Route 1: Direct link counting.} Partition the causal set into
``inside'' ($I$: past of the horizon) and ``outside'' ($O$: future).
A boundary link crosses from $I$ to $O$: it connects $u \in I$ to
$v \in O$ with $u \covers v$. The number of such links is controlled by
the thin shell of events within $\sim \lP$ of the boundary. In a Poisson
sprinkling, this shell has area $A$ and thickness $\sim \lP$, containing
$\sim A/\lP^3$ events, each contributing $\sim \mathcal{V}_{\max}$ links. The
fraction that actually cross the boundary is $\sim \lP/\mathcal{V}_{\max}$ by
isotropy. Total: $N_{\mathrm{links}} \sim A/\lP^2$.

\textbf{Route 2: Spectral (Seeley--DeWitt).} The Benincasa--Dowker
operator $\boxlayer$ (Definition~\ref{def:box}) has a discrete heat
kernel $K(t) = \mathrm{Tr}\,e^{-t\boxlayer}$. The Seeley--DeWitt
expansion gives:
\[
  K(t) = \frac{V}{(4\pi t)^{d/2}}\left(c_0 + c_1\,t + O(t^2)\right),

$R$. For a boundary at $r = r_0$, the coefficient $c_1$ picks up a
surface term $A/(16\pi^2)$. Identifying $t = \lP^2$ and entropy with
the number of independent short-time modes:
\[
  S = c_1 \times \frac{A}{4\lP^2} = \frac{A}{4G}.
\]
Both routes agree, confirming that $S = A/(4G)$ is a robust consequence
of discrete causal geometry, not an artefact of any particular counting
method.

\begin{proof}
\textbf{1.\ Heat flux:} Consider a pencil of null generators $k^\mu$
crossing a local patch of a causal horizon. The energy carried through
the patch by matter is:
\[
  \delta Q = \int T_{\mu\nu}\,k^\mu k^\nu\,d\lambda\,dA,
\]
where $\lambda$ is the affine parameter along $k^\mu$ and $dA$ is the
cross-sectional area element.

\textbf{2.\ Unruh temperature:} An observer accelerating with proper
acceleration $a$ through the vacuum perceives a thermal bath at
temperature $T = \hbar a/(2\pi k_B)$.  The physical origin is the
\textbf{KMS condition}: a uniformly accelerating Rindler observer's

fact that a node ``sees'' its own past light cone as a thermal
reservoir---the links it cannot access (beyond the Rindler horizon)
constitute missing information, and missing information has a
temperature \KC{Thm.~5.2}.

\textbf{3.\ Entropy change:} $dS = dA/(4G)$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:area-law}).

\textbf{4.\ Raychaudhuri equation:} The expansion $\theta$ of a
congruence of null geodesics measures the rate of change of the
cross-sectional area. The shear $\sigma_{\mu\nu}$ quantifies
shape distortion, and the rotation $\omega_{\mu\nu}$ measures
twisting. The Raychaudhuri equation is:
\[
  \frac{d\theta}{d\lambda} = -\frac{1}{d-2}\theta^2
  - \sigma_{\mu\nu}\sigma^{\mu\nu}
  + \omega_{\mu\nu}\omega^{\mu\nu}
  - R_{\mu\nu}k^\mu k^\nu.
\]
For a \emph{local equilibrium} horizon: (i) $\theta = 0$ at the
bifurcation point (the horizon is neither expanding nor contracting),
(ii) $\sigma = 0$ (no shear---the horizon is locally spherical),
(iii) $\omega = 0$ (hypersurface-orthogonal generators). The equation
reduces to:
$\delta A = -\int R_{\mu\nu}\,k^\mu k^\nu\,d\lambda\,dA$.

\textbf{5.\ Assembling (the Synge--polarisation identity):}
Substituting steps 2--4 into $\delta Q = T\,dS$:
\[
  T\,dS = \frac{\hbar\kappa}{2\pi} \cdot \frac{\delta A}{4G}
  = -\frac{\hbar\kappa}{8\pi G}\int R_{\mu\nu}\,k^\mu k^\nu\,d\lambda\,dA.
\]
Equating with $\delta Q$ and cancelling common factors:
\[
  \int T_{\mu\nu}\,k^\mu k^\nu\,d\lambda\,dA
  = \frac{1}{8\pi G} \int R_{\mu\nu}\,k^\mu k^\nu\,d\lambda\,dA.
\]
The key step: this holds for \emph{all} null vectors $k^\mu$ and
\emph{all} horizon patches. By the Synge--polarisation identity
(holding for all null $k^\mu$ implies holding as a tensor equation),
we deduce:
$R_{\mu\nu} + \Phi\,g_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu}$
for some scalar $\Phi$.

\textbf{6.\ Fixing $\Phi$:} Conservation $\nabla^\mu T_{\mu\nu} = 0$
and the contracted Bianchi identity
$\nabla^\mu(R_{\mu\nu} - \frac{1}{2}Rg_{\mu\nu}) = 0$ together fix
$\Phi = -\frac{1}{2}R + \Lambda$, where $\Lambda$ is an integration
constant (the cosmological constant). The result:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{R_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac{1}{2}Rg_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu}
  = 8\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu}}
\end{equation}
\end{proof}

\[

back-reaction from events that are ``used up'' in maintaining

\textbf{Step 4:} Substituting $\rho_{\mathrm{eff}}$ into the
Friedmann equation: $H^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho_{\mathrm{eff}} =

\end{proof}

\section{Topological Genesis}

\begin{theorem}[Topological Genesis \KC{Thm.~A.6--A.7}]\label{thm:topological-genesis}
Let $H = (\mathcal{C}, \covers)$ be the Hasse diagram of a Poisson-sprinkled
causal set at Planck density. There exists a critical local density
$\rho_c$ such that for any causal diamond $D$ with $|D| > \rho_c \cdot

threatens to produce a minor of $K_5$ or $K_{3,3}$ \cite{kuratowski1930}.

The unique resolution consistent with the triangle-free constraint
(Theorem~\ref{thm:triangle-free}) is non-planar contraction: the threatening
node is absorbed into a pole of a Causal Prism $K_{2,N}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\textbf{Step~1: The Ramsey bound.} For triangle-free graphs, the Ramsey
number $R(3,k) = O(k^2/\log k)$ (Ajtai--Koml\'os--Szemer\'edi). At
Planck density, a diamond of volume $V$ contains $n = \rho V$ vertices.
In a triangle-free graph on $n$ vertices with maximum degree $\mathcal{V}_{\max}$,
the independence number satisfies $\alpha \le O(\sqrt{n\log n})$ by
Ramsey theory. This forces dense neighbour-sharing: most vertices have
overlapping Hasse neighbourhoods.

\textbf{Step~2: $K_{2,3}$ forcing.} In a triangle-free graph where two
nodes $u, v$ share three or more common Hasse neighbours
$\{w_1, w_2, w_3\}$, the induced subgraph on
$\{u, v, w_1, w_2, w_3\}$ is exactly $K_{2,3}$---a complete bipartite
graph. Note that:
\begin{itemize}
  \item No edge can exist between $w_i$ and $w_j$ (that would form a
    triangle with $u$ or $v$).
  \item No edge can exist between $u$ and $v$ (again, triangle with
    any $w_i$).
\end{itemize}
The structure is \emph{forced} to be bipartite. If $u$ and $v$ share
a fourth neighbour $w_4$, we get $K_{2,4}$; a fifth gives $K_{2,5}$,
which contains $K_{3,3}$ as a minor.

\textbf{Step~3: The 4-case uniqueness.} Why is $K_{2,N}$ the
\emph{only} permitted structure? Consider the alternatives:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Add a shortcut $u \covers v$:} creates a triangle
    $u$-$w_i$-$v$. Forbidden.
  \item \emph{Add an edge $w_i$-$w_j$:} creates a triangle
    $u$-$w_i$-$w_j$. Forbidden.
  \item \emph{Split the threat by adding a third pole $z$:} creates
    $K_{3,N'}$, which for $N' \ge 3$ contains $K_{3,3}$. Forbidden.
  \item \emph{Contract the threatening node into a pole:} produces
    $K_{2,N}$ with $N \ge 3$. \textbf{This is the unique resolution.}
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Step~4: Resolution.} The triangle-free constraint forbids
all resolutions except vertex contraction. The threatening node is
absorbed into the pole of highest degree (minimising the entropy
cost). The result is a Causal Prism $K_{2,N}$
(Theorem~\ref{ax:planarity}). \KC{Thm.~A.6--A.7}
\end{proof}

inevitability}---the only mechanism by which a dense triangle-free graph
can remain planar \cite{diestel2017,kuratowski1930}.

\section{Topological Mass and Inertia}

The \emph{static} mass of a prism is the integer count $M_{\mathrm{static}} = N$ of parallel causal paths through the defect
\KC{Law~L2}. This has a direct physical meaning through
Theorem~\ref{thm:time-dilation}: each intermediate $w_i$ contributes one
unit of geodesic delay. A prism with $N = 10$ intermediates absorbs 9
more links of proper time than one with $N = 1$.

The \emph{dynamical} mass incorporates a superlogarithmic correction:
$M_{\mathrm{dyn}} = \kappa\,N\,H_N$, where $H_N$ is the harmonic number.
This arises from the cover time of a random walker confined to the prism---the
walker must visit every intermediate before exiting coherently (the
coupon-collector penalty). The full derivation appears in
Volume~II, Chapter~10.

\begin{definition}[Inertia Tensor \KC{Def.~5.1}]\label{def:inertia}
For a Causal Prism with poles $(u, v)$ and intermediates
$\{w_1, \ldots, w_N\}$ embedded in a Hasse diagram, the
\textbf{inertia tensor} is:
\begin{equation}
  I_{ab}(u,v) = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\!\bigl[\ell(u,w_i) + \ell(w_i,v)
  - \ell_{\mathrm{vac}}(u,v)\bigr]_a
  \bigl[\cdots\bigr]_b,
\end{equation}
where the subscripts index the causal directions of the deviation
\KC{Def.~5.1}.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Accumulated Delay \KC{Thm.~5.2}]\label{thm:accumulated-delay}
The total geodesic delay accumulated by a maximal chain traversing a
region of prism density $\nu$ is:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta\tau = \tau_{\mathrm{vac}} - \tau = \sum_{j} (N_j - 1),
\end{equation}
where the sum runs over all prisms encountered. In the continuum limit,
this converges to the GR time dilation $\Delta\tau = \frac{1}{2}
g_{00}\,\Delta t$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
See \KC{Thm.~5.2}. Follows directly from
Theorem~\ref{thm:time-dilation} by summing over all prisms along the
geodesic path.
\end{proof}

\section{Valence Saturation as Topological Exclusion}

Genesis creates matter, but why doesn't matter create more matter without
bound? The answer is valence saturation (Modulo~6).

Each pole of a prism $K_{2,N}$ has $N$ internal links plus at most
$\mathcal{V}_{\max} - N$ external links. Once $\deg(u) = \mathcal{V}_{\max} = 15$, the pole
is \textbf{causally saturated}: no new $\covers$-links can form. A
non-planar configuration adjacent to a saturated pole cannot be
resolved by contraction into that pole. It must either nucleate a new
prism or remain unresolved (which is impossible if $H$ is to remain
planar).

\begin{corollary}[Self-Limiting Condensation]\label{cor:self-limiting}
Prism formation is self-quenching: each condensation event reduces the
number of free Hasse links in the neighbourhood, lowering the local link
density below the Ramsey threshold for further threats. The universe
contains \emph{dilute matter in a vacuum} rather than collapsing into a
single topological defect.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Each prism absorbs $N + 2$ events and $2N$ links. The surrounding vacuum
loses $O(N \cdot \mathcal{V}_{\max})$ potential links, suppressing further Ramsey
forcing in the causal neighbourhood. The bound
$\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{prism}}(V) \le \rho_0 V / 2$ follows from the
minimum event cost per prism.
\end{proof}

permutations $(\sigma_0, \sigma_1)$ acting on the edge set:
$\sigma_0$ encodes the cyclic order of edges around black vertices
(poles), $\sigma_1$ around white vertices (intermediaries). The
\textbf{face permutation} $\sigma_\infty = \sigma_0 \circ \sigma_1$
determines the faces; the Euler--Grothendieck formula then fixes the
genus of the embedding surface:
\[
  V - E + F = 2 - 2g, \qquad F = \text{number of cycles of } \sigma_\infty.
\]
Belyi's theorem \cite{belyi1979} guarantees that \emph{every} compact
Riemann surface defined over $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ arises from such
a drawing---so the combinatorics of permutations completely classifies
the topology.

\textbf{Worked example: $K_{2,3}$ on the sphere.}
The electron prism has 2~poles and 3~intermediaries, hence 6~edges.
The intermediary permutation swaps each pair:
$\sigma_1 = (1\;4)(2\;5)(3\;6)$.

$\sigma_0 = (1\;2\;3)(6\;5\;4)$.

carefully. We get $F = 3$ independent 2-cycles, so
$V - E + F = 5 - 6 + 3 = 2 = 2 - 2g$, hence $g = 0$: the sphere.
The electron lives on $S^2$---Generation~1.
