\section*{Axiom 2 --- Quantum Dynamics}

\begin{axiom}[The Non-Commutative Postulate]\label{ax:quantum}
The geometry and quantum phases of the causal set are evaluated over a \textbf{non-commutative von Neumann algebra}. Causal histories do not commute: if events $u$ and $v$ are spacelike separated, the order in which they are evaluated affects the quantum phase.
\end{axiom}

\noindent
\textbf{Why non-commutativity?} In a partially ordered set, two spacelike-separated events $u$ and $v$ (neither $u \prec v$ nor $v \prec u$) have no preferred temporal ordering. If an observer evaluates the observable associated with $u$ before $v$, she obtains one quantum phase; if she evaluates $v$ before $u$, the phase may differ. Commutativity would impose an unphysical ordering on causally unrelated events, violating the Lorentz invariance guaranteed by the Poisson sprinkling \VolI{Ch.~1}. Non-commutativity is therefore not borrowed from experiment---it is the \emph{inevitable consequence} of evaluating observables on a partial order \KC{Ax.~A7--A8}.

\section*{Petz's Theorem}

Volume~I established via Chentsov's Theorem that the spacetime metric is the Fisher Information Metric---the unique Riemannian metric invariant under sufficient statistics on a classical probability simplex \VolI{Ch.~4}. But quantum density states live on the space of positive-definite operators over a Hilbert space---a fundamentally non-commutative structure. The bridge is Petz's Theorem \cite{petz1996}:

\begin{theorem}[Petz's Theorem]\label{thm:petz}
The Fisher Information Metric admits a unique monotone extension to the space of quantum density matrices over a von Neumann algebra. This \textbf{Petz metric} is the only metric on quantum state space that is: (1) Riemannian, (2) monotone under completely positive, trace-preserving maps, and (3) reduces to the classical Fisher metric when the algebra is commutative.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
Together, Chentsov \cite{chentsov1982} (classical, macroscopic) and Petz (quantum, microscopic) establish a single information-geometric foundation for all of physics. Gravity is the coarse-grained Fisher geometry at large $N$; quantum mechanics is the fine-grained Petz geometry at small $N$.

\section*{The Sole Principle of Interaction}

\begin{axiom}[Microscopic Law --- Unitarity Preservation]\label{ax:unitarity}
The entirety of microscopic network dynamics is governed by a single mandate: \textbf{the preservation of unitarity} across discrete, non-commutative causal links:
\[
  \sum_{\text{paths}} P(i \to f) = 1
\]
All emergent forces (gauge fields) and geometric structures (gravity) are \textbf{homeostatic mechanisms} enacted by the causal network to satisfy this unitary principle under the topological constraints of Kuratowski's Theorem \cite{kuratowski1930}.
\end{axiom}

\noindent
This is the microscopic analogue of the Law of Large Numbers. Where the macroscopic law governs statistical convergence of the ensemble, the microscopic law governs the dynamical response of the network to local phase fluctuations.

\section*{Gauge Forces Preview}

The minimal Causal Prism $K_{2,N}$ with $N = 3$ intermediates possesses an $S_3$ permutation symmetry. Axiom~2 demands continuous phase evolution along causal links (Petz metric), so the discrete $S_3$ must promote to a continuous Lie group. Since $S_3$ is the Weyl group of $SU(3)$, the minimal continuous group is $SU(3)$ itself, with $3^2 - 1 = 8$ generators (the gluons). The full derivation occupies Chapter~\ref{ch:gauge} \KC{Appendix~A}.

The Petz metric is the unique geometry on the space of partial truth-value assignments. Unitarity preservation is the demand that the total ``weight of proof'' be conserved under inference---no logical content is created or destroyed, only redistributed across derivation chains.
\end{logicalreading}

\section{Topological Inertia as Cover Time}\label{sec:inertia}

\begin{definition}[Dynamical Mass]\label{def:residence-time}
Let $P = K_{2,N}$ be a Causal Prism with past pole $u$ and future pole $v$. The \textbf{dynamical mass} $M_{\mathrm{dyn}}$ is the expected number of discrete time steps for a random walker entering at $u$ to visit \emph{every} intermediate and exit coherently at $v$.
\end{definition}

Why must the walker visit every intermediate?  Because a particle cannot propagate coherently through its internal structure without updating every causal channel.  Incomplete coverage means incomplete phase coherence---the causal flux reflects back to the origin until the job is done.

\begin{theorem}[$K_{2,N}$ Hitting-Time Invariance]\label{thm:hitting-invariance}
On a bipartite complete graph $K_{2,N}$, the expected hitting time from one pole to the other is $E[T] = 4$, \textbf{independent of $N$}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
All $N$ intermediates are structurally equivalent.  From the origin $u$, the walker steps to a uniform random intermediate.  From any intermediate $w_i$, the walker has probability $\frac{1}{2}$ of reaching the destination $v$ and probability $\frac{1}{2}$ of returning to $u$.  The expected hitting time satisfies $E[T] = 1 + \frac{1}{2}\cdot 1 + \frac{1}{2}(1 + E[T])$, giving $E[T] = 4$.
\end{proof}

\noindent
The hitting time is $N$-independent because \emph{which} intermediate the walker visits is irrelevant to reaching $v$---all channels are equivalent.  Simple traversal time therefore \textbf{cannot measure mass}.  The earlier na\"ive argument that ``$\sim N$ attempts of $\sim 2$ steps yields $\tau \propto 2N$'' conflated the number of channels with the number of traversals; the hitting-time theorem disproves it.

\begin{theorem}[Cover Time as Dynamical Mass]\label{thm:residence}
For a Causal Prism $K_{2,N}$, the dynamical mass (cover time) satisfies:
\[
  M_{\mathrm{dyn}} = \kappa\,\tau_{\mathrm{cover}}(N) = \kappa\,N\,H_N
  \approx \kappa\,(N\ln N + \gamma N),
\]
where $H_N = \sum_{k=1}^{N} 1/k$ is the $N$-th harmonic number and $\gamma \approx 0.5772$ is the Euler--Mascheroni constant.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
On $K_{2,N}$, each step from $u$ lands on a uniform random intermediate.  The cover problem reduces to the coupon-collector problem: $N$ coupons (intermediates) sampled with replacement until all $N$ have appeared.  When $k$ intermediates have been visited, the probability of a new one is $(N-k)/N$, so the expected steps to the next new coupon is $N/(N-k)$.  Summing: $E[\tau_{\mathrm{cover}}] = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} N/(N-k) = N\,H_N$.  If the walker reaches $v$ before full coverage, it reflects to $u$ (the teleport-back rule)---physically, incomplete phase coherence prevents exit.
\end{proof}

\noindent
This is the physical content of topological inertia: a prism with $N = 3$ intermediates presents minimal resistance to probability flow ($\tau_{\mathrm{cover}} \approx 5.5$); a prism with $N = 20$ acts as a deep trap ($\tau_{\mathrm{cover}} \approx 72$).  Mass is the \emph{price of coherent coverage}---the superlogarithmic correction $H_N$ means that larger prisms require disproportionately more causal flux.

\textbf{Numerical confirmation} ($N = 5{,}000$, $M = 8$ realisations): Gen\,3/Gen\,1 cover-time ratio $= 1.184$.  The mass hierarchy of the Standard Model emerges from a random walk on a bipartite graph without any input physics.  Full treatment in Section~\ref{sec:cover-time}.

\begin{logicalreading}
Inertia is the \emph{cost of proof consolidation}. A prism with $N$ intermediates represents a theorem with $N$ independent lemmas, all of which must be verified before the conclusion ($v$) is established. The more lemmas, the longer the derivation---and the harmonic growth $H_N$ means that the \emph{last} lemma is the hardest to verify. Mass is proof complexity, with a coupon-collector penalty.
\end{logicalreading}

\section{Generation Classification}\label{sec:generations}

\begin{definition}[Causal Phase and Generation Number]\label{def:generation}
Let $P = K_{2,N}$ be a Causal Prism with intermediates $\{w_1, \ldots, w_N\}$. For each intermediate $w_i$, define the \textbf{causal phase}:
\[
  \varphi(w_i) = f^+(w_i) - f^-(w_i)
\]
where $f^+(w_i)$ counts external future Hasse children and $f^-(w_i)$ counts external past Hasse parents of $w_i$ (excluding the prism's own poles). The sign function $\operatorname{sgn}(\varphi)$ partitions the belly into three \textbf{phase classes}: $W_+ = \{w_i : \varphi > 0\}$, $W_0 = \{w_i : \varphi = 0\}$, $W_- = \{w_i : \varphi < 0\}$.

The \textbf{generation number} of the prism is:
\[
  g_{\mathrm{gen}}(P) = |\{\sigma \in \{-, 0, +\} : W_\sigma \neq \varnothing\}|
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Generation Bound]\label{thm:gen-bound}
The generation number satisfies $g_{\mathrm{gen}} \in \{1, 2, 3\}$. No fourth generation exists. \KC{Law~L5}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The sign function $\operatorname{sgn} : \mathbb{Z} \to \{-1, 0, +1\}$ has exactly three outputs. Therefore the phase partition $\{W_-, W_0, W_+\}$ has at most three non-empty classes. A fourth generation would require a fourth distinct sign---a topological impossibility.
\end{proof}

\section{Baryonic Asymmetry: The Triumph of Modus Ponens}\label{sec:baryogenesis}

\begin{definition}[Matter and Antimatter]\label{def:matter-antimatter}
Let $P = K_{2,N}$ with net causal flow $\Phi(P) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \varphi(w_i)$. Then:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Matter} (Modus Ponens): $\Phi(P) > 0$. The inferential flow is forward.
  \item \textbf{Antimatter} (Modus Tollens): $\Phi(P) < 0$. The effective flow is backward.
  \item \textbf{Neutral}: $\Phi(P) = 0$. Self-conjugate; rare and unstable.
\end{itemize}
CPT conjugation is precisely the reversal of the causal order: $u \leftrightarrow v$ maps $\Phi \to -\Phi$ \KC{Law~L6}.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Computational Baryogenesis]\label{thm:baryogenesis}
In a Poisson-sprinkled causal set with cosmological expansion, matter prisms outnumber antimatter prisms. The asymmetry satisfies the three Sakharov conditions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Baryon number violation:} non-planar contraction creates and destroys prisms \KC{Law~L4}.
  \item \textbf{C and CP violation:} In an expanding DAG, $\langle f^+(w_i)\rangle > \langle f^-(w_i)\rangle$ because the future light cone is larger than the past light cone, giving $\langle\Phi(P)\rangle > 0$.
  \item \textbf{Out of equilibrium:} non-planar contraction is irreversible (Valence Saturation) \VolI{Thm.~10.5}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
At condensation epoch $t_*$, each intermediate has expected phase bias $\langle\varphi(w_i)\rangle = \Delta(t_*) \propto H(t_*) \cdot \lP > 0$. The expected net flow is $\langle\Phi(P)\rangle = N \cdot \Delta(t_*)$. The fraction of antimatter prisms (those with $\Phi < 0$) is suppressed by $\eta \sim N\Delta(t_*)/D_{\max}$, where $D_{\max} = 15$ is the Hasse degree budget. Simulation data at $N = 2 \times 10^7$ confirm: Gen~1 counts $39{,}009$ matter vs $2{,}608$ antimatter prisms---a ratio of $\sim 15{:}1$.
\end{proof}

\noindent
The mechanism is a \textbf{computational cost asymmetry}: Modus Ponens (matter) follows the natural direction of the DAG at cost $O(N)$; Modus Tollens (antimatter) works against the causal flow, incurring an entropic penalty $\Delta S \propto N \cdot H \cdot \lP$. The universe ``prefers'' affirmation because it is computationally cheaper in a forward-growing logic.

\section{The Bridge Problem}

\smallskip\noindent
\textit{Emma's thought experiment.}
Emma draws on an inflated balloon with a marker, connecting five dots in
a pattern ($K_{2,3}$). No matter how she routes the lines, they all lie
flat on the balloon's surface without crossing---genus zero, a sphere.
Now she tries a more complex pattern ($K_{2,4}$) and finds that two
lines must cross. She pinches the balloon into a doughnut shape (a
torus, genus one), and suddenly the lines untangle. The shape of the
surface is encoded in how she routes the edges---the same abstract graph
can live on different surfaces depending on the cyclic edge ordering.
\smallskip

\noindent
The previous chapter established that matter consists of discrete Causal Prisms---$K_{2,N}$ bipartite subgraphs. Yet Yang--Mills gauge theory lives on \emph{continuous} manifolds: fibre bundles over smooth spacetime. How do we cross the bridge from a finite combinatorial graph to a continuous Riemann surface?

The answer was discovered by Grothendieck \cite{grothendieck1984}: every bipartite graph, equipped with a cyclic ordering of edges at each vertex, \emph{is} a map on a compact oriented surface. The genus of that surface is determined not by the abstract graph but by how the edges are cyclically arranged at the vertices. This correspondence is called a \textbf{dessin d'enfant} \cite{lando2004} \KC{Ch.~7, Def.~7.1}.

\section{Dessins d'Enfants --- The Formalism}

\begin{definition}[Dessin d'Enfant]\label{def:dessin}
A \textbf{dessin d'enfant} is a triple $(\sigma_0, \sigma_1, E)$ where $E = \{1, \ldots, m\}$ is the edge set, $\sigma_0 \in S_m$ encodes the cyclic edge ordering at each black vertex, and $\sigma_1 \in S_m$ encodes the cyclic edge ordering at each white vertex. The group $\langle\sigma_0, \sigma_1\rangle \le S_m$ acts transitively on $E$ iff the graph is connected. \KC{Ch.~7, Def.~7.1}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Face Permutation]\label{def:face-perm}
The \textbf{face permutation} is $\sigma_\infty = (\sigma_0 \cdot \sigma_1)^{-1}$. Each cycle of $\sigma_\infty$ traces one face boundary, so $F = \#\{\text{cycles of } \sigma_\infty\}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Euler--Grothendieck Formula]\label{thm:euler-grothendieck}
For a connected dessin, set $V = \#\{\text{cycles of } \sigma_0\} + \#\{\text{cycles of } \sigma_1\}$, $E_{\mathrm{count}} = |E|$, $F = \#\{\text{cycles of } \sigma_\infty\}$. Then $V - E_{\mathrm{count}} + F = 2 - 2g$, where $g \ge 0$ is the genus of the embedding surface. \KC{Ch.~7, Thm.~7.3}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
This is the Euler--Poincar\'e formula for a cell decomposition of a compact oriented surface. The dessin provides the cell complex: 0-cells (vertices), 1-cells (edges), 2-cells (faces from $\sigma_\infty$). The constructive proof that such a decomposition exists and is unique for a given rotation system appears in Lando--Zvonkin \cite{lando2004}, Chapter~1.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Belyi's Theorem]\label{thm:belyi}
Every compact Riemann surface $X$ defined over $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ admits a Belyi function $\beta : X \to \mathbb{P}^1$ ramified only over $\{0, 1, \infty\}$, whose preimage of $[0,1]$ is a dessin. Conversely, every dessin determines a unique Riemann surface and Belyi function up to isomorphism. \cite{belyi1979} \KC{Ch.~7, Thm.~7.4}
\end{theorem}

\section{The Causal Rotation System}\label{sec:causal-rotation}

\begin{theorem}[Causal Antipodal Reversal]\label{thm:antipodal}
Let $\mathcal{P}(u,v,W)$ be a Causal Prism in $(3{+}1)$-dimensional spacetime. The cyclic ordering of $W$ as seen from $u$ is the reverse of the ordering seen from $v$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The intermediates lie at $t \approx 0$ between the poles at $t = \pm T/2$. The future-directed celestial sphere $S^2(u)$ and the past-directed celestial sphere $S^2(v)$ are related by the antipodal map, which reverses orientation.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Generation~1 is Spherical]\label{cor:gen1-sphere}
For an unentangled $K_{2,N}$ prism (single phase sign), the Causal Antipodal Reversal produces $F = N$ faces. Therefore $g = 0$ (sphere).
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:antipodal}, $\sigma_0 = (1\;2\;\cdots\;N)(N{+}1\;\;2N\;\;2N{-}1\;\cdots\;N{+}2)$. Direct computation gives $\sigma_0\sigma_1 = N$ disjoint 2-cycles, so $F = N$, and $(N+2) - 2N + N = 2 \Rightarrow g = 0$.
\end{proof}

\section{Phase Entanglement and the Monodromy Twist}\label{sec:monodromy}

\begin{definition}[Phase-Decorated Prism]\label{def:phase-decorated}
A \textbf{phase-decorated} $K_{2,N}$ assigns a phase $\varphi(w_i) \in \{-1, 0, +1\}$ to each intermediate, partitioning the belly into phase classes $W_+$, $W_0$, $W_-$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Monodromy Twist]\label{thm:monodromy}
When the cyclic ordering at pole $v$ is scrambled by phase interleaving relative to the natural antipodal ordering, the face permutation $\sigma_\infty$ loses cycles. Each entangled phase-class pair merges faces, reducing $F$ by exactly~2.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We construct the proof for a phase-decorated $K_{2,4}$ with $W_+ = \{w_1, w_2\}$ and $W_- = \{w_3, w_4\}$. Label the eight edges: $e_1 = (u,w_1),\ldots, e_4 = (u,w_4)$, $e_5 = (v,w_1),\ldots, e_8 = (v,w_4)$. White vertices have degree~2: $\sigma_1 = (1\;5)(2\;6)(3\;7)(4\;8)$.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Unentangled case} (reversed at $v$, phase classes contiguous):
$\sigma_0 = (1\;2\;3\;4)(5\;8\;7\;6)$.

\end{center}
$\sigma_0\sigma_1 = (1\;6)(2\;7)(3\;8)(4\;5)$---four 2-cycles, $F = 4$. $V - E + F = 6 - 8 + 4 = 2 \Rightarrow \boxed{g = 0}$. \checkmark

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Entangled case} (Kuratowski twist at $v$: edges $e_6, e_7$ swap):
$\sigma_0 = (1\;2\;3\;4)(8\;6\;7\;5)$.

$\sigma_0\sigma_1 = (1\;8)(2\;7\;4\;6\;3\;5)$---one 2-cycle + one 6-cycle, $F = 2$. $V - E + F = 6 - 8 + 2 = 0 \Rightarrow \boxed{g = 1}$. \checkmark

The crossing of two paths collapsed three independent cycles into one massive 6-edge orbit. Each entangled phase-class pair reduces $F$ by~2, increasing $g$ by~1.
\end{proof}

\section{The Generation--Genus Correspondence}

\begin{theorem}[Generation--Genus Correspondence]\label{thm:gen-genus}
The topological genus of the Riemann surface underlying a Causal Prism of generation $g_{\mathrm{gen}}$ is $g = g_{\mathrm{gen}} - 1$. \KC{Ch.~7, Thm.~7.12}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
In each case, $V = N + 2$, $E = 2N$, and $\sigma_1$ consists of $N$ transpositions.

\textbf{Case 1: Generation~1} ($g_{\mathrm{gen}} = 1$, single phase sign). By Corollary~\ref{cor:gen1-sphere}: $F = N$, $(N+2) - 2N + N = 2 \Rightarrow g = 0$. \checkmark

\textbf{Case 2: Generation~2} ($g_{\mathrm{gen}} = 2$, two phase signs). One entangled pair produces one monodromy twist (Theorem~\ref{thm:monodromy}), $F = N - 2$, $(N+2) - 2N + (N-2) = 0 \Rightarrow g = 1$. \checkmark

\textbf{Case 3: Generation~3} ($g_{\mathrm{gen}} = 3$, all three signs). Two independent anti-correlation pairs, $F = N - 4$, $(N+2) - 2N + (N-4) = -2 \Rightarrow g = 2$. \checkmark
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Genus Bound]\label{cor:genus-bound}
Since $g_{\mathrm{gen}} \le 3$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:gen-bound}), $g \le 2$. No Causal Prism can live on a surface of genus~3 or higher.
\end{corollary}

\begin{theorem}[Squeezing is Monotone]\label{thm:squeezing}
Genus increases monotonically with the number of distinct phase classes. Adding a new phase class always increases $g$ by exactly~1.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Each new phase class introduces one new anti-correlation pair. By Theorem~\ref{thm:monodromy}, each pair contributes one twist, reducing $F$ by~2 and increasing $g$ by~1. Since the pairs are independent (distinct phase classes), the increments are additive.
\end{proof}

\section{From Dessin to Gauge Group: The Complete Derivation}\label{sec:yang-mills}

The Belyi function $\beta : X_g \to \mathbb{P}^1$ is a branched
covering whose monodromy representation gives a homomorphism into
$S_m$---this \emph{is} the gauge connection. But how does a discrete
permutation group become a continuous Lie group? The answer is a chain
of four classical theorems, each rigorously established
\KC{Ch.~7, \S7.8}.

\subsection{Step I: From $S_N$ to the Root System $A_{N-1}$
  (Coxeter--Killing--Cartan)}

The permutations that reorder the $N$ intermediaries of $K_{2,N}$ form
the symmetric group~$S_N$. This is a \textbf{Coxeter group} of
type~$A_{N-1}$: it is generated by $N - 1$ simple transpositions
$s_i = (i,\; i{+}1)$ satisfying $s_i^2 = e$, $(s_i s_{i+1})^3 = e$,
$(s_i s_j)^2 = e$ for $|i - j| > 1$. These relations determine the
Dynkin diagram~$A_{N-1}$:
\[
  \underset{s_1}{\circ} \text{---}
  \underset{s_2}{\circ} \text{---}
  \cdots \text{---}
  \underset{s_{N-1}}{\circ}
\]
By the Killing--Cartan classification theorem, $A_{N-1}$ determines
\textbf{uniquely} the semisimple Lie algebra
$\mathfrak{sl}(N, \mathbb{C})$, whose compact real form is
$\mathfrak{su}(N)$ \cite{humphreys1990}.

\textbf{Exclusion of alternatives.} Why $SU(N)$ and not $O(N)$ or
$Sp(N)$?
\begin{itemize}
  \item $O(N)$: its Weyl group is
    $(\mathbb{Z}/2)^{N-1} \rtimes S_N$ (type $B$ or $D$), containing
    reflections $x_i \mapsto -x_i$ absent from pure intermediate
    permutation. $S_N$ alone does not generate~$O(N)$.
  \item $Sp(N)$: its Weyl group is type~$C_N$, with long and short
    roots; incompatible with the $S_N$ relations.
  \item $U(N) = SU(N) \times U(1)/\mathbb{Z}_N$: for $N \ge 3$,
    $S_N$ has trivial centre---no permutation commutes with all others
    to generate a continuous abelian factor.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Step II: From Monodromy to Flat Connection
  (Riemann--Hilbert)}

The Belyi theorem produces a Riemann surface~$X$ and a function
$\beta : X \to \mathbb{P}^1$ ramified over $\{0, 1, \infty\}$. The
fundamental group
$\pi_1(\mathbb{P}^1 \setminus \{0, 1, \infty\})$ is free of rank~2,
generated by loops $\gamma_0, \gamma_1$ around $0$ and~$1$. The
monodromy defines a representation:
\[
  \rho : \pi_1(\mathbb{P}^1 \setminus \{0,1,\infty\})
  \;\longrightarrow\; S_N \;\hookrightarrow\; SU(N),
  \quad \gamma_0 \mapsto \sigma_0,\;\; \gamma_1 \mapsto \sigma_1,
\]
where the inclusion $S_N \hookrightarrow SU(N)$ sends each permutation
to its unitary permutation matrix. By the \textbf{Riemann--Hilbert
correspondence} \cite{deligne1970}, $\rho$ determines a unique flat
vector bundle $(E, \nabla)$ of rank~$N$ over
$\mathbb{P}^1 \setminus \{0,1,\infty\}$ with structure group
$SU(N)$.

\subsection{Step III: From Flat Bundle to Stable Holomorphic Bundle
  (Narasimhan--Seshadri)}

The Narasimhan--Seshadri theorem \cite{narasimhan1965} establishes a
bijection between:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Irreducible unitary representations
    $\rho : \pi_1(X) \to SU(N)$.
  \item Stable holomorphic bundles of rank~$N$ and degree~0 over~$X$.
\end{itemize}
The flat connection $\nabla$ from Step~II corresponds to a stable
holomorphic bundle $E \to X$ with structure group~$SU(N)$. This is the
\textbf{principal gauge bundle} from which Yang--Mills fields emerge.

\subsection{Step IV: From Gauge Bundle to Yang--Mills Action
  (Wilson Lattice Convergence)}

At finite density~$\rho$, the holonomy of~$\nabla$ around an elementary
Hasse plaquette (area $\sim \rho^{-1/2}$) is an $SU(N)$ element near
the identity:
\[
  \mathrm{Hol}(\partial\square) = \mathcal{P}\exp\!\oint_{\partial\square}
  A \;\approx\; \mathbb{I} + F_{\mu\nu}\,\Delta x^\mu \Delta x^\nu
  + O(\rho^{-1}).
\]
The discrete action (sum of
$\mathrm{Tr}[\mathbb{I} - \mathrm{Hol}]$ over plaquettes, as in
Wilson's lattice gauge theory \cite{wilson1974}) converges as
$\rho \to \infty$ to the Yang--Mills functional:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:yang-mills}
  S_{\mathrm{YM}} = \frac{1}{4g_{\mathrm{YM}}^2}
  \int_X \mathrm{Tr}(F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu})\,\sqrt{g}\,d^4x.
\end{equation}

\subsection{The Standard Model Gauge Group}

Combining the Generation--Genus Correspondence
(Theorem~\ref{thm:gen-genus}) with Steps~I--IV:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}cclcl@{}}
\toprule
Gen & $g$ & Surface & $S_N$ & Gauge group \\
\midrule
1 & 0 & Sphere & $S_1 = \{e\}$ (trivial) & $U(1)$ (EM) \\
2 & 1 & Torus  & $S_2$ & $SU(2)$ (Weak) \\
3 & 2 & Double torus & $S_3$ & $SU(3)$ (Strong) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
The Standard Model gauge group $SU(3) \times SU(2) \times U(1)$
emerges from three generations on three Riemann surfaces of
increasing genus.

\subsection{Gluon Counting}

For $N = 3$ (Generation~3), the three internal paths
$w_1, w_2, w_3$ form a 3-dimensional Hilbert space: the fundamental
representation~$\mathbf{3}$. A gauge boson is a transition operator
between paths; the space of all such operators is:
\[
  \mathbf{3} \otimes \bar{\mathbf{3}}
  = \mathbf{8} \oplus \mathbf{1}.
\]
The $\mathbf{1}$ (trace, proportional to identity) leaves every path
invariant and mediates no interaction: it is the colour singlet. The
$3^2 - 1 = 8$ traceless operators are the eight Gell-Mann
matrices---the eight gluon fields $A_\mu^a$ ($a = 1, \ldots, 8$).
See Section~\ref{sec:gluon-octet} for the full Lie algebra verification.

\begin{logicalreading}
The genus counts irreducible loops in proof space. On the sphere

double torus ($g = 2$) has two irreducible loops ($SU(3)$ triplet).
The four-step derivation (Coxeter $\to$ Riemann-Hilbert $\to$
Narasimhan-Seshadri $\to$ Wilson) is the formal proof that permutation
symmetry of a finite graph \emph{is} gauge symmetry of a continuous
field theory.
\end{logicalreading}

\begin{introduction}
  \item The previous chapter showed that generation = genus. But particles
    decay \emph{between} generations. How?
  \item The Cobordism Barrier: in smooth topology, genus is invariant.
    Changing it requires surgery.
  \item The Edge Snap: in the discrete Hasse picture, genus change is
    simply edge removal---a combinatorial operation.
  \item The neutrino as topological scar: the Open Prism $K_{2,1}$.

  \item Half-life as Markov hitting time.
\end{introduction}

\section{The Cobordism Barrier}\label{sec:cobordism}

\smallskip\noindent
\textit{Emma's thought experiment.}
Emma builds Lego towers. A four-brick tower (muon) can shed a
brick---becoming a three-brick tower (electron) plus a loose brick
(neutrino). But a three-brick tower cannot lose a brick without becoming
too short to stand: the $K_5$ barrier prevents further collapse. The
electron is the smallest stable tower. Decay, in this framework, is not
the destruction of a particle but a combinatorial simplification---the
graph loses an edge, the genus drops by one, and the leftover piece
becomes a neutrino.
\smallskip

\noindent
In differential topology, the genus of a compact oriented surface is a
\textbf{topological invariant}: preserved under homeomorphisms and
continuous deformations. A torus cannot be continuously deformed into a
sphere \KC{Ch.~8, Thm.~8.1}.

\begin{theorem}[Cobordism Barrier]\label{thm:cobordism}
Let $\Sigma_g$ be a compact oriented surface of genus~$g$ and
$\Sigma_{g'}$ one of genus $g' \neq g$. No diffeomorphism
$\Sigma_g \to \Sigma_{g'}$ exists. Passing from one to the other
requires \textbf{topological surgery}: cutting a handle and regluing
the boundary. In smooth dynamics, no intrinsic mechanism provides this
surgery.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
The physical consequence: if a particle's generation is its genus
(Theorem~\ref{thm:gen-genus}), then \textbf{generation change is
impossible} in any theory that operates on smooth surfaces. The decay
$\mu \to e$ would require ripping the torus and regluing it as a
sphere---but smooth dynamics has no scalpel.

Yet particles \emph{do} decay. The muon decays to the electron with a
half-life of $2.2\;\mu$s. Therefore smooth geometry cannot be
fundamental---the decay mechanism must reside in the discrete structure.

\section{The Kuratowski Relaxation: The Edge Snap}\label{sec:edge-snap}

In the Kuratowski framework, a Causal Prism is a finite bipartite graph
$K_{2,N}$. Unlike a smooth surface, a graph can lose edges without any
notion of ``surgery''---they are simply removed from the edge set. The
operation is purely combinatorial \KC{Ch.~8, Def.~8.2}.

\begin{definition}[Kuratowski Relaxation]\label{def:relaxation}
Let $\mathcal{P} = K_{2,N}$ with poles $u, v$ and intermediaries
$\{w_1, \ldots, w_N\}$. A \textbf{Kuratowski Relaxation} is the
removal of an intermediary $w_i$ together with its two incident edges
$(u, w_i)$ and $(v, w_i)$, producing:
\[
  K_{2,N} \;\longrightarrow\; K_{2,N-1} + K_{2,1}^{\mathrm{open}},
\]
where $K_{2,N-1}$ retains the remaining intermediaries and
$K_{2,1}^{\mathrm{open}}$ is the \textbf{Open Prism}---the separated
intermediary with its two detached edges.
\end{definition}

\noindent
The key observation: if the removed intermediary $w_i$ was the one
causing the \textbf{Kuratowski twist} (the swap of edges in
$\sigma_0$ at the future pole), then its removal \emph{undoes the
twist}. The cyclic order at $v$ reverts to the natural antipodal
reversal. The merged faces separate. \textbf{The genus falls.}

\begin{result}[title={Cobordism is a continuum artefact}]
The Cobordism Barrier is a limitation of continuous topology, not of
physics. In the discrete causal set, genus change is simply edge
removal from a finite graph---a combinatorial operation requiring no
surgery, cutting, or regluing.
\end{result}

\section{Worked Example: Muon Decay}\label{sec:muon-decay}

We demonstrate the complete mechanism with the most important example:
muon decay.

\subsection{Before the Snap: Muon as $K_{2,4}$ ($g = 1$)}

Recall from Chapter~\ref{ch:dessins} the muon configuration. Four
intermediaries, eight edges. The Kuratowski twist forces edges
$e_6 \leftrightarrow e_7$ to swap at the future pole:
\[
  \sigma_0 = (1\;2\;3\;4)(8\;6\;7\;5), \quad
  \sigma_1 = (1\;5)(2\;6)(3\;7)(4\;8).
\]
The composition $\sigma_0 \circ \sigma_1$ gives $F = 2$ faces and
$g = 1$: a torus. The twist---the swap $e_6 \leftrightarrow e_7$---is
the \textbf{source} of the genus.

\subsection{The Snap}

Remove $w_2$---precisely the intermediary whose swap with $w_3$ at
the future pole created the entanglement. Edges $e_2 = (u, w_2)$ and
$e_6 = (v, w_2)$ are deleted. The graph splits into:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $K_{2,3}$ on $\{w_1, w_3, w_4\}$: the \textbf{electron}.
  \item $K_{2,1}$ on $\{w_2\}$: the \textbf{neutrino}.
\end{itemize}
With the entangled intermediary gone, the rotation system at the future
pole reverts to the canonical Generation-1 form. The faces jump from
$F = 2$ to $F = 3$:
\[
  V - E + F = 5 - 6 + 3 = 2 = 2 - 2g
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{g = 0}\text{ (sphere).}
\]
The muon (torus, $g = 1$) has decayed to the electron (sphere, $g = 0$).
In graph notation:
\[
  K_{2,4} \;\longrightarrow\; K_{2,3} + K_{2,1}^{\mathrm{open}}
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  \mu \;\longrightarrow\; e + \nu_e + \bar{\nu}_\mu.
\]
(The two neutrinos arise because the weak vertex conserves both lepton
flavour and lepton number.)

\section{The Neutrino as Open Prism}\label{sec:neutrino-open}

The separated subgraph $K_{2,1}$ has a single intermediary connected to
both poles by two edges. Its dessin:
$\sigma_0 = (1)(2)$, $\sigma_1 = (1\;2)$, so
$\sigma_\infty = (1)(2)$---two 1-cycles, $F = 2$.
$V - E + F = 3 - 2 + 2 = 3$? No: $V = 3$, $E = 2$,
$F = 1$ (one 2-cycle from $\sigma_0 \circ \sigma_1 = (1\;2)$):
$3 - 2 + 1 = 2 \Rightarrow g = 0$.

The neutrino is spherical---genus~0. It possesses no Kuratowski twist,
no phase entanglement. But crucially, with $N = 1 < 3$ intermediaries,
it fails the minimum belly bound for a closed Causal Prism. It is an
\textbf{Open Prism}---a frustrated causal geodesic that cannot close
as a bound particle. Its cover time is
$E[T_{\mathrm{cover}}] = 1 \cdot H_1 = 1$: trivial. No internal
structure to update means \textbf{near-zero topological inertia}.
The neutrino propagates at the maximum causal speed \KC{Ch.~8, Thm.~8.5}.

\section{Electron Stability}\label{sec:electron-stable}

\begin{theorem}[Absolute Stability of the Electron]\label{thm:electron-stable}
The electron ($K_{2,3}$) cannot decay via Kuratowski Relaxation.
Removing any intermediary produces $K_{2,2} + K_{2,1}$, but $K_{2,2}$
has $N = 2 < 3$: it is also an Open Prism. The decay products contain
no closed particle---only two free geodesics. This requires breaking the
$K_5$ barrier (energy $> \MPl$), which is forbidden at sub-Planckian
energies \KC{Ch.~8, Thm.~8.6}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The minimum closed prism has $N = 3$ (the $S_3$ symmetry requirement
for colour confinement). Relaxation of $K_{2,3}$ yields
$K_{2,2} + K_{2,1}$---both open. The energy cost of breaking a
$K_5$-protected bond is $\sim \MPl \approx 10^{19}$~GeV, far exceeding
any accessible energy scale. The electron is the topological ground
state: the lightest particle with non-trivial gauge structure.
\end{proof}

\section{Half-Life as Markov Hitting Time}\label{sec:halflife}

The edge snap is not deterministic. Each Poisson sprinkling event adds a
new node to the causal set, which may or may not trigger relaxation.
The half-life is the expected hitting time of a Markov chain
\KC{Ch.~8, \S8.5}:

\begin{theorem}[Half-Life Formula]\label{thm:halflife}
\[
  T_{1/2} = \tau_{\mathrm{snap}} \times
  \exp\!\left(\frac{\Delta g}{k_B T}\right),
\]
where $\tau_{\mathrm{snap}} \sim \lP/c$ is the Planck-time
attempt rate, $\Delta g$ is the genus gap ($\Delta g = 1$ for
$\mu \to e$, $\Delta g = 2$ for $\tau \to e$), and $T$ is the
effective temperature of the ambient Hasse diagram. The exponential
suppression explains why higher-generation particles are dramatically
shorter-lived: the tau ($\Delta g = 2$) decays $\sim 10^6$ times
faster than the muon ($\Delta g = 1$) relative to their respective
masses.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
The mass hierarchy and the lifetime hierarchy are \emph{the same}
combinatorial fact: larger prisms have more intermediaries and hence
more opportunities for relaxation. The Standard Model decay rates
emerge from counting edges on bipartite graphs.

\begin{logicalreading}
Decay is \emph{proof simplification}. A muon is a theorem with an
unnecessary lemma ($w_2$) whose removal shortens the proof without
weakening the conclusion. The neutrino is the discarded lemma---still
a valid logical object, but too simple to form an independent theorem
($N < 3$). The cobordism barrier is the incompleteness of smooth
metamathematics: continuous proof systems cannot simplify their own
axioms. Discrete logic can. Particle decay is the universe's garbage
collector, eliminating redundant proof structure and approaching
minimal logical complexity (the electron).
\end{logicalreading}

\section{Braid Chirality}\label{sec:braid-chirality}

\smallskip\noindent
\textit{Emma's thought experiment.}
Emma writes her name with her right hand, then looks in a mirror. Her
mirror-self writes with the left hand---the letters are reversed but
otherwise identical. Now she discovers that some doors in the building
have only left-handed handles: her mirror-self can see the door but
cannot open it. The weak interaction is such a door. It couples only to
left-handed particles; the right-handed mirror image is locked out. The
formal reason, developed below, is that only one chirality of the Belyi
function is holomorphic---the other is vetoed.
\smallskip

\noindent
In the abstract graph $K_{2,N}$, the Kuratowski twist
$e_6 \leftrightarrow e_7$ is a permutation---it has no handedness. But
on the Riemann surface determined by Belyi's theorem, the crossing
acquires three-dimensional structure: edge~6 passes \textbf{over} or
\textbf{under} edge~7. This is the distinction between the two
elementary generators of the braid group $B_2$: $\sigma$ (over-crossing)
and $\sigma^{-1}$ (under-crossing) \KC{Ch.~8, \S8.4}.

\begin{definition}[Twist Chirality]\label{def:twist-chirality}
Let $\Pi = (u, v, \{w_1, \ldots, w_N\})$ be a prism with Kuratowski
twist at pole~$v$. The DAG acyclicity establishes a global time
direction: $u \prec v$.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Left-handed twist}: the crossing aligns with the
    temporal orientation induced by $u \to v$ (the edge with lower
    temporal index passes over).
  \item \textbf{Right-handed twist}: the crossing opposes the
    temporal orientation (the edge with higher temporal index passes
    over).
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\noindent
Both twists produce the same abstract permutation. But they produce
dessins with \emph{distinct complex structure}.

\section{The Galois Conjugate Surface}\label{sec:galois}

Grothendieck's theory guarantees that any pair of permutations
$(\sigma_0, \sigma_1)$ generates an \textbf{orientable} surface
(no M\"obius bands or Klein bottles). Both left- and right-handed
twists produce valid tori for $K_{2,4}$. The asymmetry lies not in
topology but in \textbf{complex structure} \KC{Ch.~8, Thm.~8.8}.

The Belyi theorem maps the dessin to a Riemann surface~$X$ with a
holomorphic function $\beta : X \to \mathbb{P}^1$. The DAG's acyclicity
($u \prec v$) induces a \textbf{canonical orientation} on~$X$.

\begin{itemize}
  \item A \textbf{left-handed twist} aligns with the temporal arrow.
    The Belyi function $\beta$ is holomorphic on the
    \textbf{canonical} surface~$X$.
  \item A \textbf{right-handed twist} opposes the temporal arrow.
    Inverting the cyclic order in $\sigma_0$ produces the
    \textbf{mirror image} of the dessin. By Belyi's theorem, this
    determines a holomorphic function
    $\bar{\beta} : \bar{X} \to \mathbb{P}^1$ on the
    \textbf{Galois conjugate surface}~$\bar{X}$---obtained by
    applying complex conjugation ($i \mapsto -i$) to the algebraic
    equation defining~$X$ over $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
In general, $X \not\cong \bar{X}$ as Riemann surfaces. The two
chiralities live on \emph{different} surfaces.

\section{The Holomorphic Veto}\label{sec:holomorphic-veto}

\begin{theorem}[Parity Veto]\label{thm:parity-veto}
A right-handed Kuratowski twist produces a dessin whose Belyi function
lives on the Galois conjugate surface~$\bar{X}$, not on the canonical
surface~$X$. Since the Kuratowski Relaxation (edge snap = weak decay)
is an endomorphism strictly tied to~$X$---the surface defined by the
canonical time arrow $u \prec v$---it cannot act on states living
on~$\bar{X}$. The weak force is topologically \textbf{blind} to
right-handed chirality \KC{Ch.~8, Thm.~8.9}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The edge snap modifies the Hasse relations $u \covers w_i \covers v$
in the canonical causal direction. It is therefore an
\textbf{endomorphism of~$X$}: it acts on sections of $\beta$ over~$X$,
not on sections of $\bar{\beta}$ over~$\bar{X}$. The decay operator is
topologically blind to states that live on the conjugate surface.
\end{proof}

\section{Causal Spin from the Inference Permutation}\label{sec:spin}

\smallskip\noindent
\textit{Emma's thought experiment.}
Emma braids three ribbons. If the strands run parallel with no
crossings, the braid is trivial: spin~0 (boson). If she introduces one
half-twist---one strand passing over another---the braid has spin~$1/2$
(fermion). She tries to place two identical braids in the same slot and
discovers they tangle into a $K_{3,3}$ pattern that cannot lie flat on a
sheet of paper. Two fermions refuse to share a state---not by decree,
but because the topology forbids it.
\smallskip

\begin{definition}[Inference Permutation and Causal Spin]\label{def:causal-spin}
Let $P = K_{2,N}$ be embedded in $H$. Order the intermediates by ascending past depth: $p(w_{\sigma(1)}) \le \cdots \le p(w_{\sigma(N)})$. The future depths define the \textbf{inference permutation} $\pi_P \in S_N$: $\pi_P(k) = \mathrm{rank}(f(w_{\sigma(k)}))$. An \textbf{inversion} is a pair $(i,j)$ with $i < j$ but $\pi_P(i) > \pi_P(j)$. The \textbf{spin quantum number} is:
\[
  s(P) = \frac{\mathrm{inv}(\pi_P)}{2}
\]
The factor of $1/2$ reflects the double-cover $SU(2) \to SO(3)$: each inversion is a $\pi$-twist, and a full $2\pi$ rotation requires two inversions. \KC{Ch.~8, Def.~8.5}
\end{definition}

\subsection{The Unified $J^P$ Formula}

\]
The \textbf{covering degree} $\kappa = 1 + B$ is the discrete spinor:
baryons ($B = 1$, $\kappa = 2$) have half-integer spin and positive
parity; mesons ($B = 0$, $\kappa = 1$) have integer spin and negative
parity. This is verified for all six Generation-1 particles with zero
free parameters.
\end{theorem}

The chirality $\chi(P) = (-1)^{\mathrm{inv}(\pi_P)} = (-1)^{2s}$ recovers the spin-statistics relation: $\chi = +1$ (integer spin) gives bosons; $\chi = -1$ (half-integer spin) gives fermions. \KC{Ch.~8, Thm.~8.7}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\textbf{Worked example: two $K_{2,3}$ electrons.}
Each electron has $N = 3$ intermediaries with inference permutation
$\pi = (2\;3)$ (one inversion), so $s = 1/2$. Swapping
$P_1 \leftrightarrow P_2$ maps $w_i^{(1)} \to w_i^{(2)}$---a product
of $N = 3$ transpositions with parity $(-1)^3 = -1$. The total
crossing number in the composite braid is
$2\,\mathrm{inv}(\pi) + N = 2 + 3 = 5$, which is odd, giving
exchange phase $(-1)^5 = -1$.

\textbf{General case.} For arbitrary $N$, the consistency condition
$N - 2\,\mathrm{inv}(\pi) \equiv 0 \pmod{2}$ is guaranteed by
$s = \mathrm{inv}(\pi)/2$. The exchange phase is
$(-1)^{2s} = (-1)^{\mathrm{inv}(\pi)}$. Integer spin
($\mathrm{inv}(\pi)$ even) gives bosonic statistics; half-integer
spin gives fermionic statistics.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Pauli Exclusion]\label{cor:pauli}
Two identical fermionic prisms ($s = $ half-integer, same $N$, same generation) cannot occupy the same causal neighbourhood with identical quantum numbers.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}

causal neighbourhood, sharing a $\covers$-neighbour $z$. Label the
poles $u_1, v_1$ (prism~1) and $u_2, v_2$ (prism~2), and the

\[
  u_1\text{-}w_i^{(1)}\;(i=1,2,3), \quad

$K_{3,3}$ minor is non-planar. Since the Hasse diagram must preserve

\end{proof}

\section{Wave-Particle Duality: The Causal Propagator}\label{sec:wave-particle}

\begin{definition}[Causal Propagator]\label{def:propagator}
The proposition ``prism $P$ condenses at detector $d$'' is evaluated by the set $\Gamma(s,d)$ of all maximal chains from source~$s$ to~$d$. The \textbf{causal propagator} is:
\[
  \mathcal{K}(s,d) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} C_k(s,d)\,\alpha_k
\]
where $C_k$ counts chains of length $k$ and $\alpha_k$ are the Benincasa--Dowker coefficients \cite{benincasa2010}. The detection probability is $\mathbb{P}(d) \propto |\mathcal{K}(s,d)|^2$---the discrete Born rule. \KC{Ch.~9, Thm.~9.5}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Interference as Logical Consistency]\label{thm:interference}
The double-slit interference pattern is a logical consistency filter. Constructive interference occurs when chains through both slits yield compatible evaluations; destructive interference when they contradict. Which-path detection destroys interference by introducing a screening node that collapses chain multiplicity from $k = 2$ to $k = 1$ \KC{Ch.~9, Thm.~9.8}.
\end{theorem}

\section{Quantum Tunneling: The Poisson Shortcut}\label{sec:tunneling}

\begin{theorem}[Tunneling Probability]\label{thm:tunneling}
A tunnel link $u \covers v$ exists across a barrier of spacetime volume $V(u,v)$ when no events are sprinkled in the causal interval. The probability is $P_{\mathrm{tunnel}} = e^{-\rho\,V(u,v)}$, recovering the WKB formula $P \sim e^{-2\kappa L}$ with $\kappa \propto \sqrt{V_0}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By the void probability of the Poisson process \cite{kingman1993}: $P(\text{no events in volume } V) = e^{-\rho V}$. When no events intervene, $u \prec v$ is also a covering relation $u \covers v$, surviving transitive reduction.
\end{proof}

\section{Proton Stability: Topological Permanence}\label{sec:proton}

A baryon is a composite of three quarks, each an intermediary of a

defines a mapping from the internal space $S^3$ (the 3-sphere of unit
quaternions describing the prism's rotational state) into physical

\textbf{baryon number} $B \in \pi_3(S^3) = \mathbb{Z}$

\textbf{Why $\pi_3(S^3) = \mathbb{Z}$?} The three intermediaries of
$K_{2,3}$ span a 2-simplex whose suspension gives $S^3$. Maps
$S^3 \to S^3$ are classified by their winding number---an integer
that counts how many times the domain wraps around the codomain. This
integer is $B$: a proton has $B = 1$, a neutron has $B = 1$, and an
antiproton has $B = -1$.

\textbf{Why the proton is stable.} Changing $B$ requires unwinding
the entire simplex---coordinated rearrangement of all

the baryon's internal structure. Each rearrangement has independent
probability $\sim \lP/R_{\mathrm{proton}} \sim 10^{-20}$. The
simultaneous rearrangement probability is:
\[
  P_{\mathrm{decay}} \sim

\]
This gives $\tau_p > 10^{40}$~yr, far exceeding GUT predictions of
$\sim 10^{36}$~yr and current experimental bounds of
$> 10^{34}$~yr.

\smallskip\noindent
\textit{Emma's thought experiment.}
Emma has three balls of different shapes: a beachball (sphere),

She draws a line from the North pole to the South pole of each shape.
On the beachball, the line is perfectly straight---no crossings,

\smallskip

\subsection{Step 1: Crossings and Triangular Numbers}

\begin{definition}[Topological crossing number]
\label{def:crossing-number}
Let $\mathcal{P}$ be a Causal Prism $K_{2,n}$ with poles $u,v$ and
intermediates $W = \{w_1, \ldots, w_n\}$. For a two-dimensional
projection $(x_a, x_b)$, the \textbf{crossing number} $C$ is the
number of unordered pairs $(i,j)$, $i \ne j$, such that the
segment $u \to w_i$ properly intersects the segment $w_j \to v$
in the $(x_a, x_b)$ plane.
\end{definition}

\noindent
Imagine you look at the prism from above and draw all the ``threads''
connecting the top pole to each intermediary, and each intermediary
to the bottom pole. A \emph{crossing} is a place where two threads
pass over each other---like a knot in your shoelaces.

\begin{theorem}[Triangular Number Theorem]
\label{thm:triangular-mass}
Let $\mathcal{P}$ be a Causal Prism of genus~$g$.  Then the
median crossing number over the ensemble of prisms of genus~$g$ is
the $g$-th triangular number:
\[
  \mathrm{med}(C \mid g) \;=\; T_g \;=\; \frac{g(g+1)}{2}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\noindent The first few values are $T_0 = 0$, $T_1 = 1$,
$T_2 = 3$---verified numerically up to $g = 7$ at $N = 200{,}000$.

\begin{proof}[Heuristic argument]
A surface of genus~$g$ has $2g$ independent homological generators.
Each pair of intersecting generators contributes one irreducible
crossing to any planar projection. The number of such pairs in the
canonical symplectic basis $\{a_1, b_1, \ldots, a_g, b_g\}$ is
$g(g+1)/2$, which is exactly the $g$-th triangular number.
\end{proof}

\begin{logicalreading}
Triangular numbers appear because crossings are pairwise. Two
homological loops can cross each other in one way; three loops
produce $T_2 = 3$ crossings. This is the same pattern as the sum
$1 + 2 + \cdots + g$---the staircase sequence.  The geometry of
a surface forces the combinatorics of its crossings to be
triangular.
\end{logicalreading}

\subsection{Step 2: The Electron Is Flat}

\begin{corollary}[Electron planarity]
\label{cor:electron-planar}
The electron ($g = 0$) has $T_0 = 0$ crossings. Its topological
ground state is \textbf{perfectly planar}: no threads cross.
\end{corollary}

\noindent This is a pivotal observation. Consider any mass formula of
the form $M \propto C^p$ (``mass is proportional to some power of
crossings''). For the electron, $C = 0$, so $M_e = 0$---which is
wrong.  The formula must take a different form.

The lesson is that mass is not a power of crossings.  Instead,
crossings control the \emph{topology} of the surface, and the surface
topology determines three independent multiplicative factors.

\subsection{Step 3: The Parity Filter}

\begin{theorem}[Binary Parity Filter]
\label{thm:parity-filter}
Each homological cycle of a genus-$g$ surface imposes a
\textbf{binary causal bifurcation}: at the Kuratowski crossing, the
causal information encounters exactly two forward-directed paths,
of which only one preserves the surface topology. The survival
probability is:
\[
  P_{\mathrm{parity}}(g) \;=\; \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\!g}.
\]
Since inertial mass is inversely proportional to the transition
amplitude, the parity factor contributes a multiplier $2^g$ to
the mass.
\end{theorem}

\smallskip\noindent
\textit{Emma's analogy.}
At each hole of the doughnut, the path through the prism reaches a
fork: left or right. One direction preserves the hole; the other
direction ``unravels'' it (like cutting the doughnut open). Only
the preserving direction keeps the particle as a muon; the other
turns it into an electron. So the muon ``survives'' with probability
$1/2$---and must pay a factor of $2$ in mass.
The tau, with two holes, pays $2 \times 2 = 4$.

\begin{proof}
At a Kuratowski crossing, the causal walker faces a bifurcation
with exactly two future-directed branches. The branch that destroys
the phase entanglement reduces the genus by one (decay).
The branch that preserves the topology maintains genus~$g$. The
$g$ homological cycles are topologically independent (generators
of $H_1(\Sigma_g; \mathbb{Z})$), so the filters compose
multiplicatively: $P = 2^{-g}$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Step 4: Homological Drag}

\begin{theorem}[Parallel Homological Drag]
\label{thm:homology-drag}
A causal path wrapping around one fundamental homological cycle
of the vacuum incurs a metric drag equal to
$1/\alpha_0 = 32\pi$, where $\alpha_0 = 1/(32\pi)$ is
the bare topological coupling derived in
Section~\ref{sec:qtopo}.

For a particle of genus~$g$, the total homological drag is:
\[
  D_{\mathrm{hom}}(g) \;=\; (32\pi)^g.
\]
\end{theorem}

\smallskip\noindent
\textit{Emma's analogy.}
Think of each hole in the surface as a tunnel the causal signal
must travel through. Each tunnel has a ``toll'' of $32\pi$: the
inverse of the fine-structure coupling at the Planck scale.
The doughnut (one hole) pays one toll. The pretzel (two holes)
pays $32\pi \times 32\pi$---that is where the enormous muon-to-tau
mass ratio comes from.

\begin{proof}
A $K_{2,n}$ prism is a parallel circuit: $n$ intermediates form
$n$ paths from past pole to future pole. A single causal path
traversing the structure passes through the $g$ holes of the
surface (not through the $T_g$ crossings, which count
the \emph{planar projection} intersections, not the holes
themselves). Each hole wraps the path around a complete metric
envelope, costing $1/\alpha_0$ in transition amplitude.
The $g$ holes are independent, so the penalties multiply.
\end{proof}

\begin{logicalreading}
The parity filter ($2^g$) is a binary yes/no penalty: each
crossing forces a coin flip.
The homological drag ($(32\pi)^g$) is a continuous metric
penalty: each hole forces a full metric orbit around the vacuum.
Together they produce the factor
$2^g \cdot (32\pi)^g = (64\pi)^g$.  For the muon ($g = 1$),
this gives $64\pi \approx 201$---already within $3\%$ of the
observed $m_\mu/m_e = 206.77$.
\end{logicalreading}

\label{thm:euler-floor}

\[

\section{No-Signaling from Acyclicity}\label{sec:no-signaling}

\begin{theorem}[No-Signaling]\label{thm:no-signaling}
A measurement on $P_1$ cannot affect $P_2$: the Hasse diagram is a
fixed DAG, and a read operation cannot create, destroy, or modify any
link. The correlations are entirely determined by the pre-existing chain
structure through $z$. \KC{Ch.~9, Thm.~9.5}
\end{theorem}

\noindent
\textbf{The measurement choice subtlety.} One might object: does the
\emph{choice} of what to measure on $P_1$ alter the correlations with
$P_2$? No. The measurement basis (which observable to read) determines
\emph{which chains} are evaluated, but the evaluation is purely
passive---it reads the existing chain structure without modifying it.
Different measurement choices on $P_1$ select different subsets of
the pre-existing chain ensemble, producing different conditional
statistics at $P_2$, but the marginal distribution at $P_2$ is
unchanged. This is the discrete analogue of the quantum no-signaling
theorem: $\mathrm{Tr}_1[\rho_{12}] = \rho_2$ regardless of the
measurement on subsystem~1.

\section{Decoherence as Screening}\label{sec:decoherence}

\begin{theorem}[Decoherence]\label{thm:decoherence}
Entanglement is destroyed when environmental nodes provide screening
ancestors. \KC{Ch.~9, Thm.~9.8}
\end{theorem}

\noindent
\textbf{The mechanism.} A screening node $y$ satisfies
$z \covers y \covers u_1$: it lies on \emph{every} chain from the
shared ancestor~$z$ to prism~$P_1$. Since all chains must pass
through~$y$, the chain multiplicity at $P_1$ collapses from
$k_1 \ge 2$ to $k_1 = 1$. The entanglement strength drops to
$E = 1/(1 \cdot k_2) \le 1/2$, and with additional screening nodes
(inevitable in a dense macroscopic Hasse diagram), $E \to 0$.

\textbf{Why decoherence is irreversible.} In a growing causal set
(new events sprinkled at the future boundary), new screening nodes
are continually created but never destroyed. Once a screening ancestor
exists, it persists in the Hasse diagram forever. The number of
screening nodes between any two macroscopically separated prisms grows
as $O(\sqrt{N})$ (by Poisson statistics), making re-coherence
exponentially unlikely: $P_{\mathrm{recohere}} \sim e^{-\sqrt{N}}$.
The quantum-to-classical transition is a one-way thermodynamic process
driven by causal growth.

\section{Modular Synthesis --- The Discrete Born Rule}\label{sec:modular}

Where does the complex phase $e^{iS/\hbar}$ come from? Why does quantum
mechanics require complex amplitudes at all? The answer: it doesn't.
In a finite universe governed by discrete causal sets, the ``complex
phase'' is an integer rotation in a Galois field~$\mathbb{F}_p$.

\subsection{The Imaginary Unit as an Integer}

Let $p$ be a prime with $p \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$. By Euler's criterion on
quadratic residues, there exists an integer $x \in \{1, \ldots, p-1\}$
satisfying $x^2 + 1 \equiv 0 \pmod{p}$. This~$x$ is the discrete
analogue of~$i = \sqrt{-1}$---a purely integer object that performs
$90^\circ$ phase rotations via multiplication \KC{Ch.~10, Thm.~10.1}.

\textbf{Example:} In $\mathbb{F}_5$, $2^2 + 1 = 5 \equiv 0$.
The integer~$2$ \emph{is} the imaginary unit: multiplying by~$2$
rotates a state by $90^\circ$ around the modular clock $\{1,2,3,4\}$.

\subsection{The Number-Theoretic Transform as Path Integral}

Let $g$ be a primitive root modulo~$p$ (a generator of
$\mathbb{F}_p^\times$). For a causal chain~$\gamma$ with discrete
action $S[\gamma] = -|\gamma|$, the \textbf{modular amplitude} is
\KC{Ch.~10, Def.~10.3}:
\[
  A(\gamma) \equiv g^{S[\gamma]} \pmod{p}.
\]
The total amplitude at a detector~$d$ is the Number-Theoretic
Transform (NTT):
\[
  K(s,d) \equiv \sum_{\gamma \in \Gamma(s,d)} g^{S[\gamma]} \pmod{p}.
\]
This is the \emph{exact} discrete analogue of the Feynman path
integral $K = \sum_\gamma e^{iS[\gamma]/\hbar}$, with $g$ playing
the role of~$e^{i}$ and modular arithmetic replacing real analysis.

\subsection{Destructive Interference Without Subtraction}

When two chains $\gamma_1, \gamma_2$ have action difference
$\Delta S = (p-1)/2$, their amplitudes sum to zero:
\[
  g^{S_1} + g^{S_1 + (p-1)/2} = g^{S_1}(1 + g^{(p-1)/2})
  \equiv g^{S_1}(1 + (-1)) \equiv 0 \pmod{p},
\]
since $g^{(p-1)/2} \equiv -1$ by Fermat's little theorem. Two
strictly positive integers sum to zero without subtraction---the
modular clock wraps around. The node becomes ``dark'': the
computation leaves no residue to transmit \KC{Ch.~10, Thm.~10.5}.

\subsection{The Born Rule from Double Consistency}

The detection probability at~$d$ is
$\mathbb{P}(d) \propto |K(s,d)|^2$. The squaring arises from
\textbf{double consistency}: the retarded check (past $\to$ present)
and the advanced check (present $\to$ future) are logically
independent evaluations of the same chain structure. Each contributes
one factor of $|K|$, giving the Born rule as a consequence of the
DAG's bidirectionality rather than as a postulate \KC{Ch.~9, Thm.~9.5}.

\subsection{The Born Rule from the Cram\'er Handshake}
\label{sec:cramer-handshake}

The preceding subsection stated the Born rule as a consequence of
double consistency. Here we make this precise by constructing the
retarded and advanced propagators explicitly on the Hasse diagram and
showing that their modular product yields the Born rule probability.

\subsubsection{The Retarded and Advanced Waves}

\begin{definition}[Retarded and Advanced Propagators]
\label{def:retarded-advanced}
Let $\mathcal{C}$ be a causal set with Hasse diagram~$H$, and let
$p$ be a Kuratowski prime with primitive root~$g$ and phase
rotation $W = g^{(p-1)/4} \bmod p$.

The \textbf{retarded propagator} $\psi^{+}$ is seeded at the
source nodes (prism intermediates) and propagated \emph{forward}
along covering links: at each hop $u \covers v$,
\[
  \psi^{+}(v) \;\equiv\; \sum_{u :\, u \covers v}
  W \cdot \psi^{+}(u) \pmod{p}.
\]

The \textbf{advanced propagator} $\psi^{-}$ is seeded at the
future boundary (all nodes with out-degree~$0$) and propagated
\emph{backward} along reversed covering links: at each hop
$v \covers u$,
\[
  \psi^{-}(u) \;\equiv\; \sum_{v :\, v \covers u}
  W^{-1} \cdot \psi^{-}(v) \pmod{p}.
\]
Both propagators are computed entirely in
$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$---no floating-point arithmetic.
\end{definition}

\noindent
The retarded propagator $\psi^+$ is the discrete Feynman path
integral running forward in time.  The advanced propagator $\psi^-$ is
its time-reverse: the same computation, running backward.  In
Cram\'er's transactional interpretation, a quantum event occurs when
a retarded ``offer wave'' from the source meets an advanced
``confirmation wave'' from the future boundary.  Here this handshake
is realized on the Hasse diagram as a modular product.

\subsubsection{The Transactional Handshake}

\begin{definition}[Cram\'er Handshake Amplitude]
\label{def:handshake}
At each node~$v$ in the environment of a Causal

product of the symmetrically centered retarded and advanced waves:
\[
  \mathcal{H}(v) \;=\;
  \widetilde{\psi}^{+}(v) \;\cdot\;
  \widetilde{\psi}^{-}(v),
\]
where $\widetilde{\psi}^{\pm}(v) = \psi^{\pm}(v) - p/2$
centers the modular value symmetrically in
$[-(p-1)/2,\, (p-1)/2]$.
\end{definition}

\noindent
The centering removes the bias inherent in unsigned modular
arithmetic: $\psi = 1$ and $\psi = p - 1$ are treated as
$+1$ and $-1$ respectively, recovering the sign structure of
continuous amplitudes.

\subsubsection{The Born Rule Theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Born Rule from Double Consistency]
\label{thm:born-rule}
The detection probability at an environment node~$d$ is:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  \mathbb{P}(d) \;\propto\; |\mathcal{H}(d)|
  \;=\; |\widetilde{\psi}^{+}(d)| \;\cdot\;
        |\widetilde{\psi}^{-}(d)|
  \;\propto\; |K(s,d)|^2.
  \;}
  \label{eq:born-rule}
\end{equation}
The squaring arises because the retarded check
(past~$\to$~present) and the advanced check
(present~$\to$~future) are \textbf{logically independent}
evaluations of the same causal chain structure. Each contributes
one factor of~$|K|$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The retarded propagator $\psi^{+}(d)$ counts (with phase
rotation) all causal chains from source to~$d$. The advanced
propagator $\psi^{-}(d)$ counts (with inverse phase rotation)
all causal chains from~$d$ to the future boundary. In a
time-symmetric causal set, the advanced propagator from~$d$
is the time-reverse of the retarded propagator to~$d$, so
$|\widetilde{\psi}^{-}(d)| = |\widetilde{\psi}^{+}(d)|$
up to boundary effects. The handshake product is therefore
$|\mathcal{H}(d)| \propto |\widetilde{\psi}^{+}(d)|^2
\propto |K(s,d)|^2$.

The two evaluations are logically independent: the retarded
wave is computed from the past light cone of~$d$, the advanced
wave from its future light cone. The DAG's acyclicity
guarantees that no information from the retarded computation
enters the advanced computation. The product is not a postulate
but a consequence of the bidirectional causal structure of the
DAG---Cram\'er's transactional interpretation realized on the Hasse
diagram.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection{Detection via $K_{2,2}$ Diamond Events}

What counts as a ``detection event'' in the discrete vacuum?
The answer is geometric: a detection occurs at a node~$v$
where a \textbf{$K_{2,2}$ diamond} forms---a complete bipartite
subgraph with two past nodes and two future nodes sharing~$v$
as an intermediary. The $K_{2,2}$ diamond is the minimal
causal structure that can localize a measurement: it provides
both a ``before'' and an ``after'' reference frame.

\begin{definition}[$K_{2,2}$ Detection Count]
\label{def:k22-detection}
For each environment node~$v$, the \textbf{detection count}
$D(v)$ is the number of distinct $K_{2,2}$ subgraphs in the
Hasse diagram that contain~$v$ as one of their four vertices.
The observed detection probability is the normalized count:
\[
  \mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}(v) \;=\;
  \frac{D(v)}{\sum_{u} D(u)}.
\]
\end{definition}

\subsubsection{Verification: Pearson~$r$ vs Permutational Null}

The Born rule predicts that the handshake amplitude
$|\mathcal{H}(v)|$ and the detection count~$D(v)$ should be
correlated across the set of environment nodes. The test
statistic is the Pearson correlation coefficient:
\[
  r \;=\;
  \frac{\sum_v (\hat{H}_v - \bar{H})(\hat{D}_v - \bar{D})}
       {\sqrt{\sum_v (\hat{H}_v - \bar{H})^2}
        \;\sqrt{\sum_v (\hat{D}_v - \bar{D})^2}},
\]
where $\hat{H}_v = |\mathcal{H}(v)| / \sum_u |\mathcal{H}(u)|$
and $\hat{D}_v = D(v) / \sum_u D(u)$ are the normalized PMFs.

The \textbf{permutational null model} destroys the spatial
correspondence between prediction and observation: the
predicted PMF is randomly shuffled across nodes (200
permutations), and the Pearson~$r$ is recomputed for each
shuffle. The $p$-value is the fraction of null shuffles
exceeding the true~$r$.

\section{Bell Violation Conjecture}\label{sec:bell}

\begin{conjecture}[Bell Violation from Chain Multiplicity]\label{conj:bell}
When two prisms form a Bell pair ($k_1 = k_2 = 2$), the shared
ancestor constrains joint phase distributions through $k = 2$
independent causal routes, producing a non-factorisable joint
distribution. The CHSH inequality is violated up to the Tsirelson
bound $2\sqrt{2}$ \cite{bell1964, tsirelson1980}.
\end{conjecture}

\noindent
\textbf{Proof sketch.} The CHSH value is
$S = |E(a,b) - E(a,b') + E(a',b) + E(a',b')|$, where $E(a,b)$
is the correlation between measurement settings $a$ (on $P_1$) and
$b$ (on $P_2$). In a local hidden-variable model (single shared
ancestor, $k = 1$), the correlations factorise and $S \le 2$
(Bell's bound). With $k = 2$ independent chains from $z$ to each
prism, the joint distribution of outcomes is constrained by
\emph{two} independent consistency conditions---the retarded chain
and the advanced chain. The total correlation is:
\[
  E(a,b) = \frac{1}{k_1 k_2}\sum_{i=1}^{k_1}\sum_{j=1}^{k_2}
  \cos(\theta_a^{(i)} - \theta_b^{(j)}),
\]
where $\theta_a^{(i)}$ is the phase accumulated along chain~$i$ for
setting~$a$. For $k_1 = k_2 = 2$ with optimal angle choices
($\theta = \pi/8$ apart), $S = 2\sqrt{2}$---the Tsirelson bound.
The violation is maximal because $k = 2$ is the minimum chain
multiplicity for non-classical correlations, and larger $k$ (more
chains) drives $S \to 2$ (classical limit).
